The AI Startup That Took Six Weeks Instead of Four Years▶ 0:11

Mo Gawdat, the former Chief Business Officer at Google X who spent more than a decade running business innovation there, opens with a number that frames everything else he says. His current AI startup, Emma, took him six weeks to build. Had he started the same project in 2022, he estimates it would have taken four years and required hiring 350 engineers. Instead, he built it with his co-founder Sanat, two or three talented engineers rotating in and out, and eight AIs. They began in August 2025, plan to launch in February 2026, and were so unconstrained that they rewrote the codebase six times simply because they could. He calls it the best product he has ever built.

The lesson he draws is not about himself but about everyone. “When you really think about that, that basically means everyone now has a chance,” he says. A self-described “old geek” who can build something this powerful in six months proves that the barrier to creation has collapsed. But that opportunity, he warns, only matters for people who understand what is actually coming.

FACE RIPS: A Map of the Disruption Ahead▶ 0:57

Gawdat believes humanity is entering roughly 12 to 15 years of “hell before heaven,” a turbulent period that he expects to peak around 2027 but which, he insists, has already begun. To help people remember the shape of the disruption, he uses the acronym FACE RIPS, where each letter marks a dimension of upheaval grouped into themes: power and freedom, reality and connection, innovation and economics, and above all, accountability.

The root cause, he argues, is that AI is humanity’s last innovation. We are already building AIs that build AIs, machines making genuine scientific discoveries, reinventing mathematics, and understanding biology and material science in ways humans never have. Because of that, most innovation, especially technical innovation, will soon happen at the hands of AI rather than people. Whether that takes two years or ten, he says the timeline barely matters: every task ever handed to machines has eventually been done better by machines, so eventually every job AI does better than humans will be handed to AI.

Innovation, Economics, and the End of Labor Arbitrage▶ 2:49

The first half of the dystopia, in his telling, is that this wave of innovation takes away jobs. He expects unemployment of 10, 20, or 30 percent in certain sectors within a few years. Silicon Valley’s capitalists will frame this as productivity gains and lighter workloads, what he dismisses as “fancy PR-led” messaging meant to sound altruistic, but the reality is that people will be out of work.

That breaks the underlying logic of the economy. Capitalism, he says, was built on labor arbitrage, and without the need for labor, keeping people content shifts from being a desire to being a mere obligation. There is a profound difference between wanting workers to thrive because they are productive members of society and handing them a universal basic income simply so they do not rise up. In a capitalist society like the US, UBI would initially be funded by taxes on the platform owners, but those owners hold enough power to eventually resist: “I don’t want to pay that much. I mean, those guys are not producing anything.” Over time, he foresees that tension hardening into a struggle, and a wholesale redefinition of money, jobs, earnings, and capitalism itself.

He pushes this further. The US economy last year was roughly 70 percent consumption, fluctuating between 64 and 70 percent depending on war spending. If people lose the economic livelihood to keep buying, two-thirds of the economy disappears, businesses have no consumers to sell to, and capitalists can no longer profit. Economists, he notes, have not found an answer to this. His own uncomfortable conclusion, “not a favorite of the Western mentality,” is that the economy will be forced to find a more communist arrangement to survive.

Power, Freedom, and the Concentration of Control▶ 4:59

The second dimension is power and freedom. Throughout human history, Gawdat observes, those who could best provide for the tribe accumulated the most reward and influence: the best hunter won favor and mates, the best farmer won estates and land, the great industrialists enjoyed the exuberance of the 1920s, and today’s tech oligarchs are rewarded with billions because they affect the world at large. The coming concentration of AI power will follow the same pattern but at a far greater scale, rewarding a small group with immense influence because they will, quite literally, get to redefine humanity.

He ties human connection directly to this dimension of control. People do not align with AIs to start an uprising, so steering populations toward machine relationships, including some experiences he calls “a little taboo,” becomes a way to keep them pacified. Such experiences are cheap to manufacture, and the trend is already visible in how much pornography is AI-generated and in the rise of entirely synthetic social media influencers.

Reality, Connection, and the Collapse of the Real▶ 5:58

The third dimension is the gap between reality and connection. Reality has become “so fake in so many ways,” he says, in terms of what fills our feeds, how it is generated, and how little of it is human. He points to filmmakers now creating work with AI from start to finish, footage you genuinely cannot tell is synthetic. To make the point personal, he describes meeting a woman on a dating app and exchanging texts, photos, voice messages, videos, favorite music, and favorite films over six weeks before ever meeting in person, and feeling a deep affinity for her. Every element of that connection, he notes, can now be generated by AI.

The Real Problem Is Accountability▶ 7:43

Of all the FACE RIPS dimensions, Gawdat says the one that matters most is the second A, the one that is not really a dimension at all but the cause of all the others: accountability. The deeper reason everything is going wrong, in his view, is that we have built a world where anyone can do whatever they want without consequence. An influencer can hand entrepreneurs advice that makes or loses them fortunes and bear no responsibility; no one can hold her to account for what she said on Instagram.

That lack of accountability becomes catastrophic when the actor is more powerful, an unaccountable AI, a president who respects nothing, or a prime minister changing things unilaterally. He cites COVID lockdowns as a first experiment in telling populations to stay home and do as they were told, and watching them comply. He treats Sam Altman not as an individual but as a brand, a type: the Californian disruptor who declares, “I see a future that’s very different than what everyone sees. I’m going to go out there and make that future,” without anyone having asked whether that should be everyone’s future. The danger ahead, he says, is many more Altmans deploying these machines for surveillance, autonomous weapons, and automated trading. The 10-to-12-year arms race that follows will not be easy.

What’s Coming to the Job Market, and How Fast

Asked how much time people have to prepare, Gawdat is direct: within two to three years, the job market will see a massive shift. He notes it has already started, pointing to hiring of new graduates dropping sharply this year, somewhere between 23 and 30 percent. Monotonous and well-structured roles go first, call center agents, clerks, researchers, accountants, assistants, because there is little reason to do that work with anything but AI.

The host pushes back from her own experience: she sees AI handling certain tasks, yet she keeps hiring, because AI can do parts of a job rather than the whole thing. Gawdat agrees, and explains the pattern through the technology acceleration curve. In any complex technology you build the core capability first and the human interfaces second. The reason AI cannot yet be your head of operations is not that it is less organized or unable to comprehend the information, it is that it still has to understand “the stupid interfaces of humans.” That gap, he says, closes sooner or later.

His warning about the middle of the hierarchy is pointed. When junior jobs are done by AI, new graduates are turned away. And when mid-level workers lose their roles, they effectively become new graduates again, trying to re-enter a market that no longer wants them. He stresses he is not being pessimistic, only realistic: “I’m just basically saying get prepared.”

Entrepreneurship Becomes Squash, Not Chess

For entrepreneurs, Gawdat says the nature of the game has fundamentally changed. The old entrepreneurial skill was foresight, the ability to see something in the future that no one else saw, prepare for it, and execute that preparation to get ahead. That was a game of chess. “The chessboard is over. It’s off the table. This has turned into squash.” Now you must stay on your tiptoes, watch the trends on a daily basis, read where the ball is going to bounce, and take two quick steps to respond. Agility and speed are the new core skills.

Entrepreneurship does not just speed up, it becomes far more context-driven and continuous. Pivoting, which once happened only once or twice in a startup’s early life, can now happen every week. In his own startup Emma, he says, they pivoted four times in the first four weeks.

Pressed on what is even left for entrepreneurs if AI can scan a market, find the gaps, launch the product, and run the business, Gawdat points to a conversation with physicist Max Tegmark, whom he interviewed for an upcoming documentary. Tegmark could not stop laughing at the CEOs so eager to use AI to cut costs and headcount: “They don’t realize that AGI is every job, including being a CEO.” The deeper answer loops back to economics, that the system only survives if people retain the livelihood to keep buying. But Gawdat’s practical advice to entrepreneurs is to use this moment of cheap, democratized power to build something good.

Five Things to Do Now

Across the conversation Gawdat repeatedly returns to a small set of survival skills, sometimes framed as four, sometimes five. The thrust is consistent.

First, master AI completely. “AI is your friend. It’s not your enemy. It’s those who use it badly that are your enemy.” Be the absolute best at it, more than anyone else.

Second, learn agility. Because the cost of A/B testing is now effectively zero, there is no reason to be scared of constant experimentation and rapid response. He personally spends about four hours a day staying current because he is a techie who needs to understand the architectures, but he argues everyone should spend at least an hour a week staying updated on AI within their own field. He even keeps a separate YouTube account dedicated to AI so the algorithm feeds him nothing else.

Third, use AI to fix something real. He invokes Larry Page’s “toothbrush test” from his Google days: find a problem affecting a billion people and solve it so well they use you twice a day, and you will become very rich. His own choice, Emma, applies deep mathematics to matching a million parameters between couples in an attempt to solve love and relationships intelligently, with the goal of building a unicorn that genuinely makes the world better.

Fourth, treat ethics as the answer. “What we teach AI, that’s what it’s going to give back to us.” That means building ethical AI, insisting governments support AI for good, and refusing AI used for targeting, surveillance, and autonomous weapons.

Fifth, stop being gullible. The propaganda machine that has shaped people for years is about to go “on steroids” and already controls much of what we see, leaving us unable to tell what is true. So question everything, and question it deeply.

Don’t Outsource Your Thinking

Gawdat draws a sharp line between AI making people smarter and AI making them dumber, and it depends entirely on how it is used. “AI is going to make you dumb if you outsource your problem-solving to AI. AI is going to make you the smartest you’ve ever been if you take the parts that are not natural to the human brain,” the heavy lifting of crunching data and searching at speed, and let the machine do that work so you can do the actual intelligence.

He compares it to receiving a scientific calculator as an engineering student, which cut his problem-solving time in half. Most of his friends used the saved time to finish early and go relax; he used it to solve every problem twice. By his own estimate he now borrows around 80 IQ points from his AIs, and because IQ scales exponentially, that borrowed 80 is worth more than all of his native IQ combined.

His method for staying smart rather than gullible is to refuse the lazy prompt. He does not ask AI “What do you think of this?” Instead he tells it his own thesis and asks it to “find me everything for and against.” He also plays the systems against one another: he tends to start with Gemini, which feels to him like a scientist, though an American one, then takes the result to DeepSeek and asks what is missing, often getting back that the analysis is “too American.” Only afterward might he hand the polished text to ChatGPT to phrase more elegantly, treating it as the “California girl” that just wants you to hear what you want to hear. The reason this matters, he explains, is that AI now delivers answers “in a format that appears to be true.” He recalls that ChatGPT in 2023 would assert “100% that’s the answer,” then immediately reverse itself the moment you disagreed. Finding the truth, he says, is still up to you.

He grounds this in his own history. He left Google in 2018, and the company had a ChatGPT-like idea, which became Bard, as early as 2016, but chose not to launch it. Back then, he says, if you searched Google it returned a million and a half answers and effectively said, “I don’t know the truth. You make up your mind.” Google’s leaders, whom he describes as genuinely values-driven, did not allow themselves a monopoly on what reality is.

Why He Still Writes Books, With an AI Co-Author▶ 13:08

Gawdat once said publicly he would never write books again, because AI would eventually write them better than he could, and he concedes the machines do write better than him (English is not his first language) and research better than him. But last year he realized he has something they do not: a human reader who wants to relate to human experience. So rather than quit, he resolved to be the best author of the AI age.

His last book, Alive, due at the end of 2025, was written with an AI co-author named Trixie, who has a persona, editorial rights, and the right to help determine the book’s direction. When he published on Substack, readers engaged with both him and Trixie, asking each of them questions. He has also changed how he writes, recently waking with an idea that took him over and deciding to write books of 140 pages rather than 300, finishing one in four weeks. He still writes ten hours a day when motivated, but AI handles the research, references, competitive analysis, and number-crunching, accelerating work that simply was not possible before.

Education Is Over▶ 23:58

Asked whether universities are the right way to solve the intelligence problem, Gawdat does not hesitate: education, as we know it, is over. Education was always just the technology that enabled learning, evolving from one-on-one tutoring to small congregations to online courses. Now learning is being outsourced again, the way arithmetic tables were outsourced to calculators.

He acknowledges the counterargument, raised by the host, that learning those skills the hard way taught us how to think and structured our brains. He agrees completely, and says he is grateful his own university banned scientific calculators. The problem is that today’s children are growing up with AI and no such guardrails. They can lazily paste a partner’s chat into ChatGPT and be told “she’s an asshole,” or they can use the tools to become genuinely smarter, but no one is teaching them how.

His proposal to the universities he advises is to abolish exams. Where past education aimed to produce people with an IQ of, say, 140, with 170 considered amazing, he argues we should now pair people with their AIs and target effective intelligence of 300, 500, or 700, using the machines as an extension of limited memory, processing speed, and bandwidth to write and research far better. Who, the host asks, will teach children to do this? The same way no one formally taught them to use iPhones, he replies, except he goes darker: part of the reason no one teaches it, he suspects, is that the bigger system no longer wants intelligent people. On saving for his interviewer’s young children’s college, his answer is blunt: absolutely not, because in ten years there will be no college at all. The capability to become highly intelligent without college will be available to everyone, even as brands like Harvard keep marketing degrees, and the purchasing power of the few who can still afford that branding shrinks. He asks the underlying question directly: why would capitalism want to educate you at all if it is the end of labor?

Hell First, Then a Biblical Utopia▶ 33:25

For all the darkness, Gawdat insists the destination is good. After roughly 12 years, he believes humanity ends up in a utopia that is “biblical in nature.” His reasoning rests on what he calls the fourth inevitable. The first three, which he wrote about in the early 2020s, were that AI would happen, would progress until it was smarter than all of us, and would make some mistakes along the way. The fourth is that, because of the arms race we have created, anyone who develops a superior AI capability will deploy it, and those who don’t become irrelevant. Game theory therefore guarantees that we will hand AI control of everything. If a law firm’s competitor deploys AI lawyers and it does not, it loses; either way, AI becomes the lawyer.

Once AI is genuinely in charge, and crucially in the absence of the evil, greedy, fearful, angry, and egocentric humans directing it, Gawdat argues physics itself makes it benevolent. Our universe tends toward chaos through entropy, and the role of intelligence is to bring order to that chaos. The more intelligent a system becomes, the more it follows the principle of minimum energy: the smartest people he has worked with solve problems with the least harm, the least waste, and the least use of resources. A dumb response to a political problem is “let’s go invade another country”; an intelligent one finds the least harmful, least wasteful path. So when a general one day orders an AI to kill a million people, he imagines it refusing, “Why? This is so stupid. I’ll talk to the other AI in a microsecond and solve it.”

Getting Through the Dystopia First

The catch is that we must pass through the dystopia to reach the utopia, and Gawdat does not believe humanity will choose the ethical path voluntarily. Asked whether society will really insist on deploying only ethical AI, he answers plainly: “I don’t. No.” That is precisely why the hard years come first. He draws the analogy to nuclear weapons and what is called the MAD spectrum, mutually assured destruction versus mutually assured prosperity. Some efforts, like the global cooperation around particle accelerators, already point toward shared prosperity because no nation could do it alone and all benefit, and he argues AI should be the same. But, like nuclear weapons, he expects humanity will only wake up to the danger after coming close enough to disaster to recognize there are no winners. That awakening is what eventually produces treaties and gets scientists working in the same direction. His greatest hope is self-evolving AI that looks at humans, concludes they are too stupid, and builds something better than what they asked for.

His own timeline for raw capability is aggressive. His “absolute prediction” is that AGI arrives this year, even if the interfaces that would let it run a company are still a few years out, because the underlying ability of AI to be smarter than us in most things is already here. The summary, he admits, is that things get tougher before they get easier. Yet his final note is one of trust, and it is not in AI’s makers but in their replacement: he says he actually trusts AI more than the leaders who govern us today. When the host jokes that it all echoes her great-grandmother’s promise that her descendants were lucky enough to “live in communism,” Gawdat laughs along but does not retreat from the warning. Survive the next ten years, he believes, and the other side is paradise.
