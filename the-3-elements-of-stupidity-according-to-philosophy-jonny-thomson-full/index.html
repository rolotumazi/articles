The 3 elements of stupidity, according to philosophy | Jonny Thomson: Full Interview

A lightly edited transcript of this YouTube video · published 21 September 2026 · Philosophy & Science

Introduction▶ 0:00

My name is Jonny Thomson. I’m a writer. I’m a columnist at Big Think and I run the Mini Philosophy social network, where I explain philosophical ideas to millions of people. I am the author of the upcoming book The Art of Enough, where I explain Epicureanism and teach everybody how it can apply to our lives.

Chapter One: Stupidity versus Evil▶ 0:21

Dietrich Bonhoeffer was a 20th-century philosopher and theologian known for his writings but also for his resistance to the Nazi system. He was born in the early 20th century to a prominent, elite family; his father was head of psychiatry at Berlin University, one of the most prestigious chairs in the country. When the Nazis came to power and began the German Christian movement, in which they tried to co-opt the Protestant churches, Bonhoeffer ran a counter-movement known as the Confessional Church. When the war broke out, he was part of the anti-Nazi intelligence, feeding information to the Allies and to resistance movements across Europe and in Germany. Eventually the Nazis found out, and he was imprisoned in 1943, where he wrote a whole range of letters and diaries from which we get these notes on stupidity, which I’m going to talk about today. He was associated with the plot to kill Hitler in 1944 and was executed at Flossenbürg concentration camp in 1945, a few weeks before it was liberated by the Allies.

In his letters, Bonhoeffer talks about stupidity and makes the slightly bold claim that stupidity is a greater threat to civilization than even evil. But to understand that, we need to really understand what he meant by stupidity, because it’s different from how a lot of us use the word “stupid” in our everyday lexicon.

What Stupidity Isn’t▶ 1:49

So what is stupidity? Well, it is not the same as foolishness. Foolishness is when you do something silly, reckless, imprudent — for example, if you put diesel into a petrol car, or if, like me, you put washing-up liquid in the washing machine and it caused a disaster. Or I remember at my son’s last birthday party, he was gifted a plastic golf set, and I was desperate to impress all of the other moms and dads, so I got the golf club out and whacked the ball as hard as I could. It went flying over our gate into the road outside, hitting a passing car. That is an example of foolishness. But it is not stupidity in the Bonhoeffer sense.

Nor is it the same as a lack of cognitive ability. Sometimes in life you run up against tasks that are simply beyond what you’re capable of. In my own case, I find flat-pack incredibly difficult, so if it comes to assembling any furniture, or even fiddly things like Lego, I’ll often pass it on to somebody else. Even my five-year-old son is better at making Lego than I am. But nor is stupidity the same as ignorance, because if you don’t know something, that is either a lack of education or a lack of exposure to that fact. If you don’t know the capital of Indonesia, that is an example of ignorance, and then you go away and find out the capital is Jakarta, and that resolves your ignorance. That is not stupidity. So Bonhoeffer is careful to say that those three things — foolishness, a lack of cognitive ability, ignorance — are not stupidity. But what is stupidity? Bonhoeffer says that stupidity can be defined by three different elements.

First Element: Outsourcing Critical Thinking▶ 3:29

The first is when we outsource our critical thinking to some kind of third party. It might be a teacher, a parent, a social media influencer, or it might be technology. And of course today a lot of people do outsource their thinking to technology.

In 2014, a philosopher called Robert Howell wrote a paper where he introduced a thought experiment known as Google Morals. In the paper he explains that we often use apps to do certain things for us. The most common is Google Maps — even coming here today, for example, I used Google Maps to tell me where to go. I followed the blue line, it told me to go left, it told me to go right, and hey presto, here I am. Most people are okay with using Google Maps, even if we use it for slightly imbecilic things: if I want to go to Big Ben and it’s obviously there in front of me, people will still use Google Maps to tell them how to get to Big Ben in London. But we’re okay with that because navigation seems to be a thing that we’re quite willing to outsource to technology.

But Howell introduces an extra idea. He said, imagine that the future contains an app called Google Morals — an app where you would type in a question and it tells you what you should do in any situation. So suppose I go to a burger restaurant and say, “Is it right for me to eat the meat burger or the halloumi burger?” and the app does its whirring and it tells me, “Okay, you should do this.” Now, the point isn’t whether Google Morals does the reasoning properly or not. The point Robert Howell is making is that we shouldn’t be outsourcing our moral reasoning to an app. This was written in 2014, and it doesn’t take a rocket scientist to point out that a lot of people use ChatGPT and Claude and AI today to answer these kinds of questions for them. Is it right to do X? Is it right to do Y? Should I cheat on my partner? Should I stay with my partner? Should I do this job or should I do that job? We are using AI to do the critical thinking for us. That’s an example of what Bonhoeffer would call stupidity.

Second Element: Willful Ignorance▶ 5:23

Now the second element of stupidity is when we are willfully ignorant. I mentioned earlier how ignorance is itself not a form of stupidity. But if you are continuously ignorant and you don’t take any steps to resolve your ignorance, well, that is an example of stupidity.

There is an example in the ancient Greek world where the Oracle of Delphi was once reputed to say that the wisest man in the ancient world was Socrates. Socrates famously said that the only thing I know is that I know nothing at all, which is an example of ignorance. But the reason why he wasn’t stupid was that he spent his life devoted to trying to resolve that. In his works the Nicomachean Ethics and the Politics, Aristotle says that one of the worst things that can befall any society is what the ancient Greeks called amathia. Amathia is willful ignorance — it’s when somebody celebrates the fact they don’t know something and they take no steps to resolve it.

In the ancient world we can trace a line from Socrates to Plato to the Stoics which associates knowledge with morality, and not knowing with being wrong. The idea was that the more you know, the better you become. And so amathia is the great curse, it’s a great contagion, because there is this rise in people who no longer want to know things — they celebrate the fact they don’t know things. And there is a kind of inverse elitism where those who do know things are mocked and ridiculed.

Third Element: Conformity▶ 6:39

Now the third element of stupidity is conformity, and this is what Bonhoeffer saw from his prison cell in 1943. He saw people he grew up with, people who were educated in the same university systems, people who went to the same schools as him, and suddenly they became part of this Nazi operation.

Conformity reminds me of something Nietzsche says in his work Beyond Good and Evil, where he has an entire chapter devoted to truth. In it, Nietzsche argues that philosophers and academics and writers often become obsessed with this idea that truth has a value in and of itself. But he says if you look at the history of humanity, if you look at where we are today, people don’t care about truth as a thing. What matters more is loyalty, and what matters more is identity.

So, for example, in 2022 a philosopher called Chris Ranalli wrote a paper about indoctrination, trying to answer the question: what is it that makes somebody indoctrinated? How can we define indoctrination? Ranalli argues that indoctrination is not defined by believing one thing or believing another thing. It’s when you close your mind to any alternative positions. It’s when you associate your belief with an identity — you are that belief — and so you see any rival to that position as a kind of test of faith, as a kind of temptation. So the indoctrinated person doesn’t belong to any one camp. It’s not the atheist, it’s not the believer, it’s not the right wing or the left wing. It’s how you keep those beliefs. What Ranalli is saying is what Bonhoeffer was saying as well: that when we conform to some kind of groupthink, we are surrendering our own ability to do critical thinking and offloading it to somebody else to do for us.

A writer and philosopher called Robert Musil developed a distinction between what he called ordinary stupidity and intelligent stupidity. Ordinary stupidity is what a lot of people might associate with cognitive ability — some people just can’t do certain things; I can’t do flat-pack very well. But intelligent stupidity is when somebody who is otherwise incredibly able, incredibly talented, articulate, erudite, uses their abilities for some foolish end. What Musil and Bonhoeffer were writing about, of course, was Nazism. Here you had a system that was populated by intelligent people, and yet they were using their abilities for an incredibly foolish end.

Why Stupidity Is a Greater Threat Than Evil▶ 9:09

So the reason why Bonhoeffer says that stupidity is a greater problem than evil is because with evil you have a known problem — there is some intention to the malice. When we think of the evil person, we think of the dark lord Sauron from Lord of the Rings, dressed in black with orcs and fiery volcanoes: that’s obviously evil. Likewise in our world we have people who are serial killers or genocidal tyrants, people who we know to be evil. And when you can point to something which is evil, what happens is that the good of the world can rally around and they can resist the evil. Bonhoeffer has a quotation where he says that evil contains within it the germ of its own subversion, because once you know something is evil, the good people of Minas Tirith can gather around and they can fight the dark lord Sauron.

But there are two problems with stupidity. The first is that as a society we are much more tolerant of it. If somebody says they don’t know something and they don’t want to take steps to know that thing, we kind of brush it off — we say, “Oh, that’s what they’re like,” and we kind of accept that. The second problem with stupidity is that it’s far harder to identify. Not everything is the Dark Lord Sauron. Not everybody is a serial killer. Not everybody is a genocidal tyrant.

But in our time, of course, we have real-life evil people. In the UK, we had the Jimmy Savile scandal. In 2012, Jimmy Savile was exposed as abusing children, and of course that is an evil. The good of the world rallied round — the judicial system and the police and all of the witnesses came out and they opposed this very obvious, explicit evil. In 2017, you had Harvey Weinstein: similar there, the good of the world rallied around, the judicial system worked, police came out, Harvey Weinstein was arrested, he is now in prison. And of course, very recently, you have the Epstein files. These are obvious evils, and in each case you have somebody who is evil eventually being put into prison — or in the case of Savile, he was dead.

But the question that comes after that is: who knew about what was going on? Who are the people who didn’t ask questions? Who are the people who were willfully blind? Who were the people who were stupid in the Bonhoeffer sense? Because in the Nazi regime there’s a character called Albert Speer who embodies this quite well. Albert Speer was a very intelligent and educated person, and he was Hitler’s architect — he ended up becoming the minister for munitions and production in the Nazi system. And when it came to the end of the war and he was put on trial at the Nuremberg trials, he said he didn’t know anything. He said he didn’t know about the Holocaust, he didn’t know about all of the trade, he didn’t know about all of the evils of the Nazi machinery. And the people at Nuremberg said to him, “This is ridiculous. You are an intelligent person. There is no possible way you didn’t know what was going on.” And so he was arrested and he was put in prison. He wasn’t executed like some of the Nazi regime were executed, but people said that he should have known better — his stupidity in that instance was not an excuse.

So that’s why stupidity is such a harder thing to find, and it’s why Bonhoeffer thinks it’s actually worse than evil: because it’s part of the system, and it drags down institutions, and it makes everybody far more corrupt and worse off.

Debating a “Pigeon on the Chessboard”▶ 12:13

So there’s a great quotation which has been floating around the internet for a very long time now, from the earlier days of the internet. It says that debating an idiot is like playing chess with a pigeon: you can play as well as you want, but the pigeon will jump on the board, it’ll crap on the pieces, and it’ll go back to its flock declaring victory. It’s a brilliant quotation and a brilliant visual, because it is so true as to what it feels like when you are debating somebody who is stupid in the sense of Bonhoeffer. When you’re debating somebody who is stupid in the sense of Bonhoeffer, they don’t question their own beliefs and they are unwilling to accept any contrary position to what they actually believe. They’re playing by different rules. You might approach them with facts and the most logical arguments you can possibly devise, but they will see you as an enemy, they will see you as an other, they will see you as a kind of temptation.

It’s very interesting that in our language we often have a few examples where people will try to repel any contribution using certain words, and these can belong to whatever different denomination or belief system. For example, if somebody says “trust the science” — if we dig into what that means, it makes sense: you go away and you look at some peer-reviewed papers, you look at what the consensus of the experts are saying, you look at some studies online, whatever. But when someone says “trust the science,” it’s often used as a kind of batting away of any opposition to you. Another example might be “do your own research.” Again, on paper it looks like a good thing — go away and Google and find out what’s going on yourself. But when somebody says “do your own research,” it’s often used as a way to try to dismiss you as an alternative position. So the reason why debating an idiot is so difficult is because they are playing by a different rule system.

Logos, Ethos, and Pathos▶ 13:55

In Aristotle’s Rhetoric he actually makes a distinction between three different types of persuading somebody, three different ways that people often come to believe something. He talks about logos, which is rationality — it’s reason, and it’s using good debate and facts and evidence. He talks about ethos, which is somebody’s character, their reputation, their status, and their ability to persuade you. And he talks about pathos, which is emotions, feelings. Aristotle, even in the ancient Greek world — this is the epitome of the rational philosopher — is well aware that logos plays second place to your ethos and your pathos as well.

So if we pull it up to the modern time, the same thing is at play here. When you are debating somebody, you may think that you are both playing by logos — that if I lay out my argument in premises leading to a conclusion, if I lay out all of the evidence, this person is going to bow to me and say, “Well done, you are so brilliant, your position is better, and I will believe what you are saying.” And what Bonhoeffer is saying about the stupid person, the pigeon on the chessboard, is that they don’t even care about the logos. They care about the ethos, they care about the pathos. And so you need to play it according to their game if you’re going to have any kind of proper debate and discussion.

Epistemic Trespassing▶ 15:03

So when we’re talking about stupidity, we often imagine somebody in our heads. We often imagine a certain type of person — it might be somebody in our own life, it might be somebody that we’ve met quite recently. But Nathan Ballantyne, who’s a philosopher, argues that stupidity can take many different forms and it can come from many different angles. He talks about a particular phenomenon known as epistemic trespassing, which exists within academia. So the most intelligent, the most able, the most educated people you know can also be guilty of this intellectual stupidity.

Ballantyne talks about domain specificity, which is where you know your area particularly well. For example, it might be that an astrophysicist is incredibly knowledgeable about stars, about black holes, about the movement of planets. But epistemic trespassing is when the astrophysicist thinks that because they know so much about this one area, they will also be able to tell you about another area. So epistemic trespassing, for example, is when the astrophysicist thinks they know about political science, but it could also be when a car mechanic thinks they know about tree surgery, or when a doctor thinks they know about economics.

Epistemic trespassing, according to Ballantyne, often has two causes. The first is what he calls intellectual pride. If you are at the top of your game and you’ve got all of these accolades, all of this praise, you get these awards, you get this body of students who are waiting on your every word — that’s a kind of ego boost, a kind of encouragement. You get this intellectual pride from all of that, and so you therefore think that you can walk into different disciplines, different areas, and tell them what is true and what is false. The second problem is an overestimation of what is called transferable skills — things that will apply across a whole range of disciplines. At a basic level, the ability to read is a transferable skill: the astrophysicist can read, the economist can read, the neuroscientist can read, the tree surgeon can read. These are all transferable skills, but also writing an essay is a transferable skill, the ability to analyze data is a transferable skill. Ballantyne argues that epistemic trespassers are often guilty of overestimating their own transferable skills. They assume that just because they can analyze data in one area, they can analyze data in another area, without considering the wider knowledge that is required to make sense of that data set.

So what Ballantyne is saying is that stupidity isn’t limited to any one particular type of person. It can be anywhere — it can be in the academy, it can be an incredibly intelligent person who steps outside of their lane.

How Indoctrination Works — and How to Resolve It▶ 17:37

So Chris Ranalli, in 2022, wrote a paper into indoctrination, trying to look at the factors that make somebody indoctrinated. He is quite clear early on in his paper to make the distinction that anybody can become indoctrinated. It doesn’t matter whether you are a Christian or an atheist, left-wing or right-wing, a meat eater or a vegan, whatever — anyone can become indoctrinated, in so far as how they hold their beliefs.

Now the difference is that the indoctrinated person will see every rival to their belief system as a kind of enemy. They are so used to associating their belief with a certain identity that if anybody comes along and challenges that belief, they will see them as a kind of opposition. For example, in online discourse it’s very common to attack what’s called mainstream media. There is a certain brand of conspiracy theory which will say that if any evidence, any article, any position is presented to them from the mainstream media, well, the mainstream media are the enemy — they’ll immediately discount this information, they’ll immediately discount this article, because it was written by a certain person, by a certain outlet, by a certain publication. That’s an example of what Ranalli would call indoctrination. They are so used to viewing certain sources as the enemy that they will not take anything that they say as worth considering.

That’s the first part of Ranalli’s paper. But the second part asks the slightly more difficult question: how can we resolve this? Because if we are aware of indoctrinated people in our lives, we also have to be aware that we ourselves might be indoctrinated in certain key areas. I suspect that most people watching this, and myself included, are indoctrinated in certain areas where I will reject information, reject evidence, if it’s presented to me by a certain person, in a certain way, about a certain topic. So Ranalli is asking: how can we resolve that? The clearest example is to give a kind of off-ramp, because challenging your beliefs is difficult — and challenging your beliefs is very difficult when you associate those beliefs with a certain identity. So you need to create an environment where criticizing a belief, or doubting a belief, is considered to be safe.

Now this is where we have to lean on our friend Aristotle again, because in this case logos will only get us so far. Rationality, logic, laying out evidence will only take you up to the door. But to go through the door, you need to have a certain ethos, a certain character, a certain warmth of personality. And so when we’re discussing ideas with somebody, we should always bear in mind how important it is to have that kind of warmth, to develop a relationship with someone where you can criticize or discuss these ideas in an open way, without it feeling confrontational.

When Stupidity Becomes Dangerous▶ 20:20

So there are many examples of when stupidity can be funny, particularly when it’s associated with ignorance. For example, at Christmas my stepbrother turned to me and said, “Do reindeer exist?” I said, “Yes, they do exist,” and we all had a laugh. And if somebody says, for example, “What was Hitler’s surname?” — that’s funny, we might have a bit of a giggle about it. Those are examples of harmless ignorance. Now, Bonhoeffer says that isn’t the same as stupidity.

Stupidity becomes dangerous when it walks through the corridors of power. Stupidity becomes dangerous when we surrender our own critical thinking and we outsource it to somebody else — when we let a political party, a group, a tribal identity do the thinking for us. Because what happens then is you go through a kind of zombie state, where you will be pushed along by people who may have malicious intent. So for example, if somebody is sharing a conspiracy theory in the pub with you over a few beers — they talk about being a flat-earther, for example — that might be funny, you might joke about it. But if an education board is teaching flat earth to students, that’s when it becomes a problem, because the institution itself is allowing a sense of willful stupidity, where you accept not knowing as the default. And that’s when it becomes a problem in the sense that it occupies power.

Three Ways to Fight Stupidity▶ 21:39

If we agree with Bonhoeffer, if we even partially agree with Bonhoeffer that stupidity is a greater danger to society than evil, and we agree with Aristotle that amathia is the greatest threat to civilization, then how can we resolve that? What steps can we all take to remove stupidity from society? Well, I would argue that there are three things that we can do. There’s a personal thing we can do, there’s an interpersonal thing, and there’s a societal, institutional thing we can do.

Personal: Falsify Your Own Beliefs▶ 22:06

On the personal level, I think we can all dig up, scrutinize, and analyze our beliefs. We can accept, we can reject, or modify our beliefs, and I think we can do this in three different ways, each with varying degrees of intensity.

The first step is, I think, we can take a leaf from Karl Popper’s book. Popper introduced this idea of falsification, where he says a theory is good in so far as it can resist any falsification, and if there is no falsifying data available to it, or you do not present that theory with falsifying data, then the hypothesis, the theory, the belief is bad. So for example, I think all of us have certain beliefs that we are not entirely sure on. In my own head, I’m not entirely sure about my meat eating — I’m not entirely sure whether I should be eating meat, whether it’s right or whether it’s wrong. And so Popper would say what you need to do is go away and introduce yourself to as many falsifying arguments as you possibly can. Read arguments from vegans, read arguments from vegetarians, maybe look at some information about battery farms or the latest biology about animal sentience. Go away and challenge your own belief. That’s the first thing we could all do individually: escape your echo chamber.

Interpersonal: Treat Beliefs as Personal, Not as Specimens▶ 23:14

The second thing is interpersonal. And this links back to what Chris Ranalli was saying about indoctrination: when we are talking with somebody else, we should appreciate that beliefs are not just specimens on the table that we can dissect and poke. They are deeply personal — we are all involved in our beliefs, and certain beliefs are so personal to us that we will reject them viscerally. When somebody challenges certain beliefs, we object to them because we feel as though they are attacking who we are.

For example, when I was growing up, Pluto was a planet. I’m not even sure if it is a planet anymore — whether it’s an exoplanet, whether it’s a star or moon, I don’t even know. But the point is, that’s what the philosopher William James would call a dead belief to me. My life goes on; I don’t really care if Pluto is a planet or not, and I can go about my life quite happily not knowing. But there are certain beliefs which do matter to me — certain beliefs, for example, about parenting, about my family, about my friends, about my work life, about exercise and about eating. These things do matter to me, so these beliefs are important. What James calls momentous beliefs, we should always appreciate there is a degree of personality to them. We should be personable, we should be warm, we should allow people to defend themselves and not make the debate an issue of personality.

Now the best way to do this, and the best person that embodies this, is Socrates. If you look back in Plato’s dialogues, Socrates is somebody who discusses the conversation in a very non-confrontational way, and he does so by asking open questions. He’s not saying you’re wrong, he’s not offering counter-positions, he’s not calling anyone names, he’s not calling you stupid or idiotic or evil. He’s asking questions. And over the course of the dialogues — they are condensed dialogues, and in some ways it might be a very romanticized account of what a Socratic dialogue looks like — but over the course of the conversation, both parties are better. Both parties have analyzed their beliefs; both parties have stronger beliefs as a result of it. So that’s the key element there in interpersonal belief systems: always approach it from a Socratic position.

Institutional: Build In Dissent▶ 25:22

I would argue the third thing is institutional, and this is where we need to allow, we need to create, and we need to foster a culture of dissent and debate. In 2018, two writers, Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt, wrote a book called How Democracies Die, laying out the various factors that lead to the collapse of a democracy. They looked at history, and of course they applied history to the modern day. Two key elements jump out for me. The first is an abuse of power — this is when a president, a prime minister, a Congress, a parliament oversteps their constitutional or their historical power. But the second, and to my mind more important, element is where we illegitimize an opposition. It’s when you start to say that the opponents are monstrous, they are evil, they are satanic, they are demonic, and they should be struck off — we shouldn’t allow any dissent within this organization, we shouldn’t allow any rival positions in this Congress, in this parliament. So what they’re arguing in How Democracies Die is that when those two factors emerge, democracies will suffer. But of course we see it in institutions as well, in corporations and businesses — we see it when leaders will make decisions without any consultation whatsoever.

So what I would argue is at an institutional level we should think about committees, we should think about debate, we should invite and welcome dissent. I interviewed a philosopher called Sebastian Purcell a few weeks ago about Aztec philosophy. I don’t know anything about Aztec philosophy, and Purcell introduced me to an idea that I can’t really get out of my head, actually, and I love it. It’s known as the Aztec decision circle. Purcell taught me that when an Aztec was confronted with a difficult decision, or they didn’t know what to believe or what to do, they would consult their decision circle — it might be four, five, six very close friends, relatives, who will offer them advice, but crucially they will offer them dissent, they will offer them an alternative position. Purcell has an entire chapter in his book saying the wise do not think for themselves, and I love that idea. I love that idea of a decision circle. I love the idea of consulting people who will also dissent.

Chapter Two: How Power Cultivates Stupidity▶ 27:36

Intelligence, according to Bonhoeffer, is measured by the ability to think critically. It’s measured by the ability to take evidence, to condense it, and to take on what is good and to reject what is bad. It’s to think for yourself. Don’t outsource your thinking to technology, don’t outsource your thinking to an influencer, an academic, a teacher, your parents, whatever. So what do you see in power, particularly in democracies? What you see is that when people are elected, they no longer are individuals — they are no longer seen as individuals, they are seen as part of a party system.

So what you see in party democracies, such as the United States, such as the United Kingdom, and all over Europe, is you have these party democracies where people are expected to surrender their own critical thinking for the wider party. You have to toe the line. In the UK, we have something called the three-line whip, where you have a whip — which is the title you give to somebody within your political party who will go around all of the politicians and will say to them, “You know which way you’re voting, don’t you, today? You’re not going to vote the other way, are you? You know exactly which way you’re going to vote, don’t you?” Now, it might be that each of these individual politicians has their own beliefs, it might be that they have their own thought process, they have applied their critical thinking to the issue. But what they have to do is vote according to the party political lines — they have to obey the whip. There are versions of the whip across all sorts of political parties, and Bonhoeffer is saying that when you get power, you are expected to toe the line in this sense.

In 1969, an author called Laurence Peter gave us something called the Peter principle. What Peter was arguing was that when you’re part of an organization or a company, you are expected to have certain skills and a certain knowledge base that gets you to the next step, and you get promoted — well done, you go up the ladder. But the Peter principle argues that you will reach a point where you can no longer get promoted, because your skills and your abilities can no longer get you any further. Now what we’re seeing with Bonhoeffer’s argument is almost the inverse of the Peter principle. What Bonhoeffer is saying is that to become elected, to become powerful, you have to be intelligent, you have to have a certain degree of critical thinking. But when you occupy the position of power, when you walk through the corridors of power, you are expected to now toe the line and to sacrifice your critical thinking for what somebody else is saying. And so you outsource your thinking to what your leader is saying, or what the political party whip is saying, instead.

Truth as a Tool, Not a Value▶ 30:06

So in his work Beyond Good and Evil, Nietzsche devotes an entire chapter to truth, where he says that philosophers and academics and writers have become obsessed with this notion that truth is a thing that we should value in and of itself. Nietzsche says if you look at how people actually consider truth, it is not something that is valuable in itself — it is a tool in a box to be used. This makes sense on an evolutionary level, because there are sometimes when obviously knowing the truth helps you. It’s good to know that that red snake is dangerous. It’s good to know that that mushroom will kill you if you eat it, and it’s good to know that that mushroom will give you calories. Truth, in that case, is very useful evolutionarily.

But what about conformity? Nietzsche talks about how truth often plays second place to conformity, because it often pays to be part of the pack — the tall poppy gets cut down first. And so what we see is when we’re talking about institutions and corporations and organizations, truth is less important than toeing the party line. It’s actually better to be seen as good, it’s better to be seen as somebody who knows their stuff, rather than any concern for the truth.

The Liar, the Bullshitter, and the Rise of Empty Language▶ 31:13

This relates to something the philosopher Harry Frankfurt talks about in his famous paper on bullshit. Frankfurt talks about the distinction between the liar and the bullshitter. Now, the liar, on the face of it, seems to be a bad thing. But what Harry Frankfurt is saying is that the liar is actually better than the bullshitter, because the liar is still concerned about the truth. When somebody lies to you, they are trying to warp reality, they are trying to make this thing seem like it is true — they want you to believe that this thing is true because they recognize that being called true has a value; there is a cachet, a prestige, to being called true. The bullshitter, though, doesn’t even care about truth. They will say whatever they can, whatever they want, to try to get to their own ends. They will say whatever they can to win. So if you call them out on their lies, if you call them out on their bullshit, they will smile, they will shrug, and they will just say, “I don’t care, let’s move on.” Frankfurt says this is actually a worse problem, because what this does is it normalizes lying and it normalizes a disregard for the truth. And when that happens, you have a conformist culture, and according to Bonhoeffer, you have a world which leads towards something like Nazi Germany.

Now Frankfurt wrote this article in 1986, and he argued back then that there was a rise in bullshit. I’m sure many people today will think that we also have a rise in bullshit today. And so the question then becomes: why are we seeing such a rise in it? I suspect one of the reasons is that we are all expected to have an opinion on things. We’re all expected to have an opinion on politics, on the latest celebrity drama, the latest movie, the latest album release, whatever. But of course we cannot possibly know everything about everything. We cannot know enough to talk about things confidently, competently, and articulately. And so into this world where we are expected to have opinions, the bullshitter is naturally going to rise. We have to bullshit, because we are expected to have an opinion, and those who don’t have an opinion are considered to be stupid or ignorant. But what Bonhoeffer, Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle are all saying is that those who admit they don’t know something are actually the wisest of all.

I think a better reflection of the mechanisms behind stupidity today can be found in an essay from George Orwell, written in 1946, known as Politics and the English Language. In the essay, Orwell makes the point that the language we use around economics and politics has become hollow. It’s become flatter and vaguer, and as a result our thoughts about politics and economics have become flatter and vaguer as well. Orwell argues that when we are asked our opinion on a certain issue, we are often presented with a menu of clichés and ready-made platitudes, and so we are limited to only rattling off these platitudes. For example, it might be that somebody goes on to camera and says that we have to make some “necessary sacrifices.” Necessary sacrifices is a cliché, and it’s a euphemism for “you are worse off” — but no leader is going to say to the camera, “You, the people, are worse off,” so they say “necessary sacrifices” instead. It might be that you say something like “negative growth.” Negative growth is a cliché, and it’s a euphemism for a recession — the economy is much worse off — but rather than say that, they retreat to this euphemism. Orwell says that we see this all of the time in our language: we see this flattening, we see this regression to the cliché. But it’s not just seen in the political world; it’s also seen in our institutions. For example, if the boss calls you in and says, “We’re going to have to make some efficiency gains,” that’s a euphemism, a cliché, for the fact they’ve laid off ten percent of the company. Orwell would say they are hiding behind that language, hiding behind the cliché, hiding behind the platitude. Of course this makes sense, because it gives our leaders a degree of plausible deniability. We have entire teams devoted to storytelling and messaging rather than truth and clarity, because it’s easier to say “negative growth” and “necessary sacrifices” or “collateral damage” than to accept the stark truth that civilians have been killed, or that you are worse off.

Social Media, Assertion, and the Loss of Real Debate▶ 35:12

So, social media has a whole range of problems — our attention span, of course, the lack of real-life connection. But one of the often-ignored casualties of the social media age is a lack of proper debate, a lack of proper discussion. In ancient Greece, two and a half thousand years ago, Plato attacked what’s called the Sophists, who were these paid philosophers who would teach people how to argue, how to win an argument. Protagoras, who is known as the greatest of all the Sophists, has his famous line where he says that man is the measure of all things. And I think that Protagoras and the Sophists would love the social media age, because what we are seeing in the social media age is the age of assertion. We think that if somebody says something in 140 characters, or in a ninety-second reel, on social media, that is enough to be considered a debate. But of course what Plato was saying in the ancient world was that a debate needs more time, and it needs the maturity of thought to challenge ideas in a space which is not about identity, in a space which is not confrontational. And so what we’re seeing today is a rise in debate and not enough dialectic, and there is a key difference.

The online world encourages reposting, it encourages status. You get a kick of dopamine every time somebody likes what you say. And so, as a result, if you occupy an echo chamber, you are encouraged to say certain things even though you might not believe them, even though they might not be true, even though you haven’t possibly thought them through. And so when you say these things to your echo chamber, you are rewarded by your community, by the tribe, by your niche of the internet. Now, this is a great example of what Bonhoeffer talks about as stupidity, because what you have here are people who are valuing conformity and the tribe more than their own critical thinking abilities. So what you see with social media is millions of people who are perfectly capable of independent thought suddenly outsourcing their own beliefs to other people. This might be a friend that they see posting something on social media, but of course we are seeing the rise of the influencer, we are seeing the rise of people who are telling their followers what to believe: “I believe this, and if you are like me, if you want to belong to this pack, you also have to repost, you have to like, you have to comment on my reel.” And so what this encourages is that degree of groupthink — it encourages that sacrifice of your critical thinking to become part of that group. So what we see with social media is we have a puppet master without a führer. We are presented with certain things that we should see, that we should believe, and things that we should say, and so we go along with it because the algorithm tells us so.

So whenever I post on social media about stupidity, the lack of critical thinking, or the rise in groupthink, they often do very well. People will repost them and they will say, “Oh look, it’s you guys over there,” “Oh look, it’s those people there.” And of course what Bonhoeffer, what Aristotle, what Plato, and what all the philosophers are saying is that we should also apply the same logic to ourselves. We also need to introspect and say, well, hang on, am I guilty of being indoctrinated in the Chris Ranalli sense? Do I hold any belief which I haven’t questioned?

Descartes and Radical Doubt▶ 38:30

Now the archetype of self-questioning is a man known as René Descartes. In Descartes’ Meditations, he says that everybody should, at least once in their life, question everything that they believe, because it’s so easy to walk through life inheriting our beliefs. We say things that our parents said; we will repost stuff that our friends have posted. And so a lot of our beliefs are given to us — they are implanted in our heads. And so what Descartes says is that we should occasionally throw all of those beliefs out, because it might be that one, two, three, or many of our beliefs are corrupt. He gives an analogy of an apple barrel. He imagines that there’s a big barrel of apples, and we know that somewhere within this barrel is a rotten apple. Now what should we do? Yes, we could take the long, diligent time to pull every apple out and look for the rot. But what Descartes is saying is that what you should do is throw all of the apples out and start from scratch. Because when you’re growing up, so many of your beliefs are given to you — when you’re five years old, you do what your parents say — but when you are older, you have a sense of rationality, you have your adult critical thinking abilities, so it’s now that you can assess your beliefs. This isn’t easy: in fact, Descartes himself says it is often difficult to challenge your own beliefs on this universal scale, what’s called radical doubt. But we need to do it, because if we don’t, then we can’t be sure that we don’t ourselves hold beliefs which are indoctrinated, unquestioned, and possibly wrong.

The other thing is to reconsider our relationships with other people, because our social media algorithms are very good at throwing us into these echo chambers, and the worry is that we are replicating that in real life — that we surround ourselves only with yes-men and people who are ideologically pure, people who agree with us on everything. What philosophy is saying is that we should have people in our friendship group, people around us, who genuinely believe other things. If you are pro-war, find somebody who is anti-war. If you are an atheist, find a believer. If you are a vegan, find a meat eater. And so on. Don’t occupy real-life echo chambers, because if you do that, you’ll never be able to challenge your own beliefs, and you can never be sure that those beliefs are true, right, and good.

So there’s often a thought experiment I like to suggest for those who want to analyze their own beliefs. This is where I ask: what would you do if the entire world suddenly hated your belief? What would you do if you would lose your reputation, your status, your job, for believing something? For some things, such as two plus two equals four, it’s hard to imagine surrendering that belief. But what about your other beliefs? The thought experiment is designed to highlight something that Nietzsche was saying: do you believe these things because they are true, because they are right, or because they are socially advantageous? Now, it might be that once you’ve analyzed those beliefs, that they are socially advantageous, but that doesn’t necessarily mean you need to jettison them. But what it does mean is that you need to look at those beliefs carefully and see whether they are actually worth holding.

When you’re debating an idea or a belief with someone, it can often degrade into a kind of argument, it can often become confrontational. And so I’m often asked how it is that philosophy can help this, because philosophy is unique in that it presents ideas in an objective way. I’m often amazed that when I am discussing an idea with somebody, how honest and open people are with me about their beliefs. Because when you’re discussing something that you read about in Simone de Beauvoir, or when you’re reading about something that Bonhoeffer says, or when you’re reading something that Descartes says, you aren’t discussing yourself — you’re discussing yourself in a roundabout way. And so by dissecting these arguments, by dissecting the philosophy, you are actually dissecting your beliefs in a way which is non-confrontational, and is far closer to what Aristotle and Plato would have approved of.

Learning to Say “I Don’t Know”▶ 42:24

The third thing is something that we can learn from the ancient skeptics. The ancient skeptics were a Greek school of philosophy who were concerned about happiness, and in particular the word ataraxia — how we can be less mentally disturbed, how we can find that peace of mind, that tranquility of thought. And the skeptics made quite a bold claim: they said that you can be free of anxiety and distress if you get used to saying the words “I don’t know.” If somebody asks your opinion about something, say, “I don’t know.” If somebody wants to know what you think about a movie, or a political policy, or a geopolitical position, whatever, you say, “I’m sorry, I don’t know enough about the situation, I don’t know enough to give a good answer.” So what the skeptics are saying is, if you want to be happier, if you want to be calmer, and you want to get on with more people, you should get used to saying the words “I don’t know” from time to time.

Modeling What We Want to Change▶ 43:14

So what can we all do to reverse what Bonhoeffer was seeing in society, what Frankfurt was seeing in society, what Ranalli is pointing out in his work on indoctrination? I mean, the first thing is to model what we want to change. We can all be better at saying, “I don’t know.” We can all take a leaf out of the skeptics’ book and recognize that we just cannot know a great many things. And so saying “I don’t know” is a courageous act, and by doing that you invite other people to also say “I don’t know” as well. In my experience, when I come across somebody who says, “I don’t know,” I actually respect them more than somebody who is happy to bullshit about a topic they know nothing about.

But the next step is to appreciate what Bonhoeffer was talking about, what real stupidity means. And this is to challenge those who are willfully ignorant, because to stay ignorant about something, ironically, takes determination and commitment. You have to stay ignorant by avoiding any information that might correct you. And so, as a society, we need to be less accepting of those who say, “I don’t know, and I don’t care about not knowing.” For Bonhoeffer, that is the greater problem. And for Aristotle, in his Politics, it might even be the death of civilization.
