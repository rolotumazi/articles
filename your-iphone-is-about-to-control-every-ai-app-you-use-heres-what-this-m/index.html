Apple’s Neglected Software Play

Everyone thinks that Apple lost the AI race, but what if they’ve been playing a different game all along? And what if we are going to hear about it at WWDC? Notice I didn’t talk about Mac minis. It’s become obvious to anyone in the open claw agent universe that Mac minis are the key to running on-prem AI if you are a small-time developer and you don’t want to get a big Nvidia GPU at all. They are already winning there. In fact, Mac minis are sold out of many places. That is not what I am talking about.

What I am talking about is the idea that Apple has a neglected software play that ties into their massive install base on the phone, and that they have an advantage because OpenAI is starting to stumble. If you start to play it through, OpenAI has had to refocus. There are rumors that they are starting to go a little slower with Jony Ive on this hardware play. They’re struggling to get it to market. It’s not as easy as they thought. They’re working on devices, but nothing is out yet. Meanwhile, they’re having to refocus their software footprint because of the traction that Anthropic is getting in the enterprise revenue space. That’s why OpenAI is killing Sora, and that is why OpenAI is trying, according to rumors, to get to a super app that has Codex and ChatGPT in one thing on your desktop.

All of that leaves more room for Apple, more room for AI on the phone, and more room especially post open claw for agentic AI on the phone. Who is better positioned to grab that than Apple? The WWDC announcement, if we parse it correctly, helps us to see where Apple is going and the opportunity that they have. This takes some thoughtfulness. We’re going to go through it and actually diagnose where Apple is going, what we can infer and why, and then what we can do to think ahead as strategists, as builders, just as people who are thinking about agents and AI in the space, so that we’re ready for where phones and software and AI agents are going in the second half of 2026. It’s going to be a massive multi-billion-user shift that we’re all going to go through, and we’ve got to get ready for it.

Signal One: Siri Becomes a Standalone App▶ 1:56

Here’s how we’re going to do it. I’m going to take four key signals for WWDC. I’m going to break out the strategic intent for each, and I’m going to explain what each tells us about where Apple is going. I know we’ve heard this before, so stop me if I’m wrong. You get the idea. They’ve said Siri’s going to get better, but there are concrete signals that they’re actually finishing up the transformation that Tim Cook has been eyeing for a while. According to Mark Gurman, who leaked this in Bloomberg and who’s been a very reliable source for Apple’s roadmap in the past, Siri is going to get a standalone app in the iPhone.

The thing that matters about that is that Siri is going to become a ChatGPT-like experience, where you are going to be able to natively on the iPhone talk to Siri, chat with Siri, have multimedia experiences with Siri, and so on. Because this is Apple, what this means is the chat experience isn’t limited to the app. Right now, you have to open up Gemini or open up ChatGPT to have an AI experience. But because Apple controls the entire stack for the phone, which is their whole advantage, Apple can have you ask Siri and get into the chat experience from any app. They have embedded layered options that you don’t get any other way. Craig Federighi actually hinted at this last year when he talked about the idea that they want Siri to be a place where you can get things done regardless of where you are on the phone, not something that is just a standalone where you have to go here to chat, which is what ChatGPT and Gemini have been.

So I think the vision for Siri that is starting to come out through WWDC here is that you have a compelling standalone experience for people who want that, but in addition, you have ambient intelligence in the phone, which is something that we’ve talked a lot about, and I think we’re going to start to see it unveiled when WWDC comes around.

Signal Two: Agentic Interfaces and App Intents▶ 3:42

The second big piece I want to call out is that we are starting to see agentic interfaces built by Apple into the ecosystem that all of us as builders are going to be able to access. This is going to be a huge deal because it’s going to start to differentiate apps on the phone very, very rapidly. In a world where there are hundreds of thousands of GitHub stars and demand for open claw, you do not want to be the app that is left behind here. What they are going to launch looks a lot like app intents. I believe that is what they’re going to call it. It is going to basically be a way to communicate clear intent into an application for remote interaction. The team at Apple in Cupertino is rumored to be working with some of the big names of tech who do big, highly used apps, like Amazon and Uber, to start to get some demo apps on stage for this.

If you are building and you are interested in getting your app onto the stage, the best thing you can do is to start to play around with the idea of app intents and look at how you would prep your app for that. Maybe you’re not going to get early alpha access before WWDC, but you can be thinking now about what it looks like if someone communicates an ask to Siri and you want to hook into an app intent and go find a price comparison on a shoe on Amazon. That’s the kind of thing that is going to be very, very easy to do once Apple launches all of this in the summer.

Another great example, if you’re looking for specific use cases to get the juices flowing: Apple is the most widely used camera in the world. You can do photo editing with app intents very, very easily. It’s not hard to imagine a world where you can just ask Siri and say, “Hey, apply this cinematic filter. Please clean up the three faces. Please crop this other face,” and then it just does it. The world that Apple is envisioning looks a lot like the world that ChatGPT was saying they would build for us in 2025. Only they didn’t get there fast enough, and they don’t have the durable 1.5-billion-user install base that the iPhone has. They’re trying to build their way there. Apple has for free what ChatGPT is spending billions to get to with Jony Ive right now, and it looks like Tim Cook and team have woken up and are starting to think about how they actually leverage that. That is a sleeping giant that you don’t want to bet against.

Signal Three: MCP Integration▶ 6:08

Now, this is a really interesting one. MCP integration is also supposed to be launched at WWDC. MCP integration is meaningful because Apple has a tradition of not being very nice about plugins — sometimes literal plugs. Do you remember the plug wars of Apple, where Apple had a special connector for a long time, and there were EU court cases? Apple is opening up to MCPs. What that means is that anything with an MCP server is going to be easier to plug into the ecosystem, easier to make tool calls from, and easier to get into the phone.

If that becomes something that Apple just builds at the core, meaning you don’t have to maintain your MCP support separately, then instead developers can use Apple’s system-level integration to handle MCP. Apple will handle the protocol, Apple will handle security and compatibility, and all of that. That is a huge breakthrough, because it means that Apple is basically taking 1.5 billion users, many of whom have been on ChatGPT free, and saying, “These people deserve tool access and agentic AI as well.” MCPs are a big part of how you do the tool calls to get there, and it’s going to be a massive breakthrough.

Signal Four: Choosing Gemini Over Anthropic▶ 7:11

Last but not least, I want to talk about the implications of the widely publicized decision by Apple to go with Gemini and not Anthropic for their foundational large language model partner. Apple has not built a big large language model. They are not a hyperscaler. They have not invested there, and they need that capability on the phone, and they know it. So they went with Google, and we now have the parameters of what that’s going to look like.

I think the architecture is going to split out really cleanly and make sense from a privacy-first perspective. Apple is going to have a very small single-digit-billions model that they have trained to run on their silicon. That is their model to handle private data. It will be running effectively on your phone, and they’ll be able to make those claims that your data never leaves your phone. When you want to do more complex reasoning, when you want to go to the web, when you want to do deep research, you are going to be seamlessly switched over without any branding, all white-labeled, into the Google family of models. Google will do some fancy auto-routing and send you to the right model in the background so that you get advanced intelligent support branded as Apple but really run by Google.

The Tool-Calling Implication▶ 8:15

Now, there’s an implication here for folks who have been following AI for a while, and it’s fairly technical, but stay with me. Google has not been as good at tool-calling harnesses as Claude has, or even as OpenAI has with Codex. What that means is that you are picking a model provider who has been a little bit late to the game on the harness side, and therefore you are expecting more single-use agent sessions. When we’re talking about a world where your open claw can call many different tools and come back and do a lot of agentic work for you, not very many people are necessarily choosing to power that with Google. If they do, they have to make sure that their tool-calling harness is something that Google is going to play nicely with and reliably invoke the tools and go after it.

There are a lot more people who are using a ChatGPT LLM or who are using Claude. I’m not saying nobody does — there are definitely people who use Google for this. I’m not saying Google can’t call tools; don’t misunderstand me, it certainly can. It’s compatible with MCP. I’m saying that if you look at where the momentum of the hyperscalers is, both OpenAI through Codex and Claude across all of its range of models are focused on reinforcement learning against a particular harness that enables them to do very advanced tool calling for long-running tasks. It looks like Apple is saying, “We can tolerate that not existing on our iPhone.” Instead of thinking of the iPhone as a place to run those long-running tasks, maybe they’re envisioning that will live on the Mac mini, and then you don’t have to use that for the iPhone. I think it’s worth calling out, though, because fundamentally what they’re envisioning is more of a personal-computing single-task agentic future versus a very complex, maybe developer-friendly or professional-worker-friendly multi-step agentic workflow on the iPhone.

Strategy Layer One: Controlling the User Interface▶ 10:07

If you put these four breakthroughs together, I think the strategy Apple is playing becomes very clear. Strategy layer one is controlling the user interface. Apple wants to see Siri as the default conversational agent, the default door to AI for most of their 1.5 billion users. That is the thing Tim worries about: he worries about the iPhone being displaced from a brand perspective, and he worries that he will be displaced, which is a tremendous compliment to AI, by the way, because the iPhone has been considered an absolute lock from a distribution perspective. So the fact that Tim is worried tells you how powerful AI is as a distribution experience. He wants to actually get Siri to the point where he hedges his risk and is able to provide a default experience that many of his users will find acceptable, so they’re not going to go and look at other AIs, and they’re not going to displace the brand value of Apple along the way.

Strategy Layer Two: Investing in the Apps Ecosystem▶ 10:58

Layer two is an investment in the apps ecosystem. Apple famously made their phone valuable because they turned on the ecosystem, and the phone became more and more densely packed. I think we’ve all seen the hardware photos where you see how many different hardware devices of the 2000s got packed into one device with the iPhone — you had keys, you had car keys, you had a camera, you had GPS, you get it. It all gets packed into one device. Part of how they did that was sensors plus an app ecosystem.

In this situation, the app ecosystem is something that they want to open up in a controlled way, in an Apple walled-garden way, to AI agent-friendly developers. I think that’s very interesting, because the recent moves that Apple has been making have actually been very anti-vibe-coding. Famously, they tried to go after Replit a couple of weeks ago. They are pushing back on tools that people use to code on the phone, and they are trying to push instead, it looks like through WWDC, an Apple-blessed way of development that is not vibe coding.

Vibe coding is what widens out the developer pyramid. You have maybe trained engineers at the top, but you’re going to have 100 million, 200 million, 300 million people who are able to build stuff in vibe code who are never going to be trained engineers. It’s very interesting to me that Apple seems to have said, “Our official response will be: if you’re an engineer, if you can badge in, if you can get approval to access our tool sets, if you’re a registered developer with Apple, then we’ll give you our approved agentic-friendly frameworks, and you can work with them, and yes, that will include MCP. If you are a vibe coder, we don’t like the security risks that that comes with for our users, we don’t like the way that risks our walled garden, and we’re just going to cut you off.” It remains to be seen if that’s the right call. That is cutting out potentially hundreds of millions of builders who now have to meet a much higher bar. I am unclear on where that all is going to land, but it’s worth naming as an explicit trade-off Apple is making.

Why Is Apple Late Again?▶ 13:03

Now, the obvious question: why is Apple late again? Because that’s part of what we have to be honest about. Apple telegraphed a lot of this back at WWDC ‘24 — at least the large parameters of the vision — and then they just didn’t deliver. In fact, they have faced a false advertising lawsuit because they were unable to deliver. There have been personnel changes at Apple. It has not been a good ride for a couple of years. And now, apparently, they’re finally ready to talk about it more publicly, more explicitly, and they’re still not going to actually launch some of these agentic pieces, or so the rumor goes, until September, October, somewhere in the fall.

If that is the case, then they are playing right into the usual Apple playbook. Everyone who’s an Android user will tell you Android gets stuff faster, Android often has better RAM, a lot of the technical specs are better, but you don’t get the Apple ecosystem. So where Apple typically likes to be with this kind of technology is second or third in line, but with incredibly deep integration and a seamless customer experience. That is what broke after 2024 when they launched something originally with Apple, and we all said, “Well, Siri’s terrible. This whole AI promise isn’t there. What is going on?” That is what they are trying to repair now.

So the big question, the executive-level question Tim has to face, is whether this catch-up is going to work. Is he going to protect the Apple brand and the Apple distribution footprint by actually making Siri smart, by actually making the ecosystem AI agent-friendly, so that not just Apple but everybody can build agents on the phone? I don’t know. It’s an interesting question. But that is the question he has to contend with, because he cannot afford another WWDC where they make big promises and they’re late or never on delivery.

Google’s Strategic Play

Because you know who’s not going to stop shipping? Their frenemy Google. Google shipped agentic features faster. Google’s UI automation approach, where Gemini just uses computer vision and recognizes buttons and navigates for you, works today on any app. You don’t have to wait around for developers to adopt a framework. That’s fragile, though. Vision-based approaches don’t last; they’re somewhat brittle, but at least it works. At least Google shipped it, and it’s out there in the ecosystem. Apple, as usual, is taking a more defensible and slower approach. They’re trying to say that agentic approaches should be hardwired in, that developers should adopt it, that you have a technical integration that enables a reliable, persistent grab of the app intent. Very classic Apple move, but they have to execute well this time.

Now, if we’re looking at Google more strategically, what’s the play here? I don’t think the play is Google’s rumored billion-dollar-plus deal with Apple. That is not why Google is doing this deal. I think the real game here is that Google knows Apple has to play ball with somebody when it comes to advanced LLMs. Apple doesn’t have one. Apple’s not getting one any time in three years. They’re rumored to be working on one, but they’re a ways away. It’s not easy to catch up at this point, if it’s possible at all. Google wants the signal from inference workloads on the phone, and they definitely do not want ChatGPT or Anthropic to get that signal. That is a competitive play they’re making beyond Apple. They want, when someone has a complex ask, to be able to learn — even if anonymously — from that query and come back and be smarter about it.

This gives them a much wider install base. They’re not just dealing with how it works on Android devices now, which are obviously Google-powered. They can also look across the entire ecosystem and have essentially 100% of the install base to look at from a mobile usage, app usage, and AI usage perspective. That is a very valuable thing for Google to have, and that is worth much more than a billion dollars to them. Apple is smart enough to know this, and the deal is described as temporary for a reason. The question, again, is whether Apple can actually play catch-up here, because I think if there’s anything harder than figuring out how to pivot your strategy on hardware and ecosystem for something you’ve nurtured for 20 years, it’s figuring out how to hyper-scale into a race with large language models that are at the frontiers they’re at, at the cost they’re at.

Apple actually does have the cash to play in this game. They have hundreds and hundreds of billions of dollars in cash lying around. But dollars don’t buy expertise. Dollars don’t necessarily buy their way out of the memory problem that everybody is facing. Dollars don’t necessarily put GPUs in the ground for data centers. Dollars don’t necessarily equate to incredible talent working against a common mission. If you doubt that, just ask Meta versus Anthropic and see who’s shipping on LLMs. Meta paid a lot of money, but we haven’t seen a lot of results from them lately.

The Player We Haven’t Mentioned: Samsung

There’s one other player here we haven’t mentioned who’s going to be affected by WWDC, and that’s Samsung. I know — Samsung. Samsung is in the mix here because they are the ones who have the flagship thousand-dollar-plus devices that Google tends to launch their latest agentic features on. Google does price-tier here: the fancier phones get the better agentic features in the Android ecosystem. If you are trying to reach the billions of people who want a mid-tier phone, who can’t afford a thousand bucks, and you want to give them an agentic experience, Apple suddenly has a very interesting play on the table here. Because Google has been leaning on that fancier-phone play, Apple could bring a basic phone to market — which they already have a history of doing — stick some agentic features into it, and steal a march on the Android ecosystem by basically offering a bottom-of-the-line-for-Apple, mid-tier phone with agentic capabilities that essentially outperforms anything but a thousand-dollar-plus Samsung phone.

Samsung has to be a little bit worried about that, because they’re the ones who have benefited from Google getting close to them on the Android ecosystem and being the OEM for Google. If they get into a position where, in the Android market for that aspirational middle-class user globally, they are not the ones who are first looked at, that’s a big benefit for Apple, and it’s a risk that Samsung carries more than Google does. Google has so many markets they can play in, so many options; they can start to pivot their strategy, and Samsung still has to hold the bag.

What You Do: The Developer’s Opportunity

And now we get to what you do. Where does this leave us? From a developer perspective, I think it’s pretty obvious. I have painted where Apple is almost certainly going with WWDC. This is a builder’s opportunity. You can go out there and lead the way when it comes to MCPs. Go out there and lead the way when it comes to app intents. Very obvious building plays.

If you’re leading a product or engineering organization, the question for you becomes: do we have an agentic-first app concept, or is the app basically just deterministic with a light chatbot over the top? Because so many apps are the latter, and you need apps to start to be the former. You need apps to start to be agentic-first, and that takes, frankly, some advanced product thinking before we get to WWDC, before you get the announcement and everybody jumps in and starts to race for it.

What You Do: Practicing Delegation▶ 19:40

Last but not least, if you are not a developer and you’re not leading a product or engineering organization, this is an invitation to you and a reminder that the world is moving to delegation. The world is moving to agents doing work for us. That’s not going to change. In fact, it’s going to become more and more the case over the second half of the year. We’ll see it on the Android ecosystem, we’re going to see it on the Apple ecosystem, and you need to start practicing now what it looks like to delegate.

What does it look like to make sure that you think first, “Can an agent do this work?” if you’re a knowledge worker, and not second? What does it look like to say, “Hey, I don’t know how to do this, but maybe it’s a claw away, it’s a ChatGPT away. I can just type a question into Gemini and I get my answer.” Because so many times I will get questions from people in my life who are like, “Hey, can you give me this answer?” And I just do my default. I go straight over to an AI and I say, “Hey, what’s the answer here?” I don’t do it irresponsibly. I don’t do it because I believe they aren’t a source — I check my sources, I make sure that it’s correct, but that’s where I start. Then I come back, and inevitably, one, I’m correct; two, it took me very little time; and three, I wonder, why didn’t you do that to start with? That is the default we have to start to reach for — not just for general knowledge questions, but for how we delegate.

The Real Headline▶ 21:05

Now, I guarantee you that when WWDC comes around, all of the newspapers are going to tell you — I can rip it right from the headlines — they’re going to say, “Siri is now a chatbot.” That’s going to be the headline. It’s the obvious one. No, that is not the correct headline. The correct headline is that Apple is starting to open the ecosystem to agentic intent. Apple is starting to open up and allow agents to operate across the phone. And the trade-off is that Apple wants to make sure that they are the disintermediated system of record that users and brands trust for a phone experience.

That is the long play. The long play is to protect the iPhone. So all of this at WWDC should be read in terms of: we have a 1.5-billion-user install base, and we want to make sure they still see the iPhone as aspirational, as high quality, as something that belongs in the agentic era. That is the play that they have to make. That is the much more useful, correct, and strategic headline that we should be reading in June, even though I think we’ll be reading about Siri. Best of luck. I hope Siri does actually get smarter this time, frankly, because I’m tired of a dumb Siri. Cheers.
