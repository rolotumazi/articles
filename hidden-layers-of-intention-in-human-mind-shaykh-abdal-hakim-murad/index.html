Continuing the Book of Intention▶ 0:00

We continue with Imam al-Ghazali’s book on intention, sincerity, and truthfulness. Having completed in our previous session the fifth and final bayan, or exposition — the last subsection of the first of these three parts, the Kitab al-Niyya, the Book of Intention — that’s the longest of the three sections, and we started to realize why, particularly with that fifth chapter, where he gets into some of the paradoxes and the psychological depth that necessarily have to be broached if one is going to understand what’s going on when we intend something.

We reminded ourselves that ours is a religion not so much of law and rules as of dispositions of the heart, which are uplifted and shaped by those rules but which simultaneously condition the quality of those rules. Actions are judged only by intentions — that is the prime, determining hadith of Islam, one that the Imam loves to cite in many places, not just in this book. We saw also in the previous session the complex way in which this turn within, this interrogation of our reasons for doing things, humanizes us and makes religion more obviously adapted to the frailty and the complexity of our humanity.

We saw how the Imam discussed ways in which it can be better, religiously, for us to sometimes take time off, or to do something that doesn’t seem terribly important, if we have a strong and sound reason for doing that in a halal way — better, say, than praying two additional raka’at or an optional fast of some kind. It’s very important that we understand that religion is not a kind of mechanism, not a kind of cosmic, robotic system of rewards, actions, and consequences, but something that seeks to bring out, discipline, uplift, and illuminate that which is most profound in our human self.

I think one of the things that has emerged from the treatment so far is the way in which religion, because it inculcates an alertness to our reasons for doing things, also helps us with that thing we nowadays rather frivolously call mindfulness. This is a religion of scrutiny of the self — call yourselves to account before you yourselves are called to account — not just in terms of “have I been performing good deeds,” but, as we’ve seen, inevitably, “have I been performing those good deeds for the right reasons.” Everything is about an interiorization.

This was one of the most extraordinary transformations that the Sira brought about, the prophetic revolution, because the pagan Arabs were really not interested in why they were doing things, in intention or sincerity. They just did things for pride, honour, wealth, revenge, tribal solidarity. The idea of a subtle and ongoing struggle with the self, an attempt to determine one’s intention and to create a fertile soil for good intentions, is something you could not imagine in the case of Abu Lahab or Umayya ibn Khalaf or those jahili stalwarts. It was part of the prophetic revolution. Throughout, the Imam has been giving us Qur’anic verses and hadith that show how fundamental this revolution is to the way we practise our religion.

A New Chapter: The Merit of Ikhlas▶ 4:26

So let’s move on now, inshallah, and see what wonders are in store. The previous section was quite captivating, but the Imam is now moving on to the second chapter: concerning ikhlas — its merit, what it truly is, and its degrees. Ikhlas is usually translated as sincerity.

The first section is on the merit of ikhlas, and he begins, as always, with the revealed texts: “They were commanded only to worship Allah, sincere for Him in their religion.” “Truly, for Allah is the pure religion.” “Save those who repent and put things right and hold fast to Allah and make their religion sincere for Him.” And: “Whoever hopes for the meeting with his Lord, let him do good deeds, and let him not associate any partner in the worship of his Lord.”

This last is narrated in a sound hadith which gives us the context for the revelation of this particular verse on ikhlas — it’s specifically about somebody who is acting for God but still loves to be praised for it.

Hadith on the Merit of Sincerity▶ 6:46

And so we get into the hadith. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, said that there are three things in which the heart of a believing Muslim will not be deceived — will not be disappointed — and the first of them is sincere action for Allah. The hadiths go on: Mus’ab narrated from his father that his father had thought he possessed a certain merit above those Companions beneath him. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, said that his community is helped through its weak people, through their prayers, their sincerity, and their worship. This has a number of different versions; the famous one is in Imam al-Bukhari: “Are you given provision and victory by any other than the weak amongst you?” There is this idea of the presence of the weak — who tend to have the better intentions and the purer prayers — being a kind of guarantor for the rest of the community. In every Muslim community there is an unknown elite of often quite ordinary-seeming people who have this ikhlas, and it is because of their presence that Allah defends that community.

Allah says: “Ikhlas is one of My secrets. I have hidden it away, placed it, entrusted it to the hearts of those of My servants whom I love.” It was said: do not be so worried about the slightness of your actions, but be worried about whether they are accepted. And the Prophet said: “Be sincere in what you do, and even a little of it will be enough for you.”

There is another hadith: “Every servant who is sincere in his action for Allah for forty days will find the wellsprings of wisdom flowing from his heart to his tongue.” In other words, as well as knowledge there must be wisdom, which is the application of that knowledge to the real circumstances of the world. And that wisdom comes about not so much through learning as through sincerity — a directed and sound heart.

The Three Who Will Be Judged First▶ 11:29

Three people will receive the first questions on the Last Day. A man to whom God had given knowledge: Allah asks him, “To what use did you put your knowledge?” He says, “I used to stand with this knowledge in prayer at length, at night and during the day.” Allah says, “You are lying,” and the angels say, “You are lying — what you really wanted was that people should say, so-and-so is an alim.” And that has indeed been said of him.

Another man, to whom Allah had given wealth: Allah says, “I gave you this blessing — what did you do with it?” He says, “Oh my Lord, I used to give it by night and by day.” Allah says, “You are lying,” and the angels say, “You are lying — you really wanted people to say, so-and-so is generous.” And that was indeed said.

And another man, who was killed in the path of Allah: Allah asks him, “What did you do?” “Oh Lord, You commanded jihad, and I fought until I was slain.” Allah says, “You are lying,” and the angels say, “You are lying — you just wanted people to say, so-and-so is courageous.” And that was indeed said.

Abu Hurayrah said that the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, then drew, as it were, a line on his thigh with his finger and said, “Oh Abu Hurayrah, those are the first people to be touched by the Fire on the Last Day.” The narrator of this hadith once went in to see Muawiyah and narrated it to him, and he wept until his life almost came to an end.

What This Hadith Teaches: Humility and the Benefit of the Doubt▶ 15:06

So the Imam has begun with this very clear hadith about three areas in which people have convinced themselves that they are sincere: a scholar, a wealthy man who was generous, and a warrior who was courageous. But it turns out that what they did was worthless. Ikhlas, like niyya, is about the interiorising of our concerns for our actions, and among the many ethical benefits this yields is, first of all, puncturing our pride. We might think, “I’ve prayed so many times, I must be a really great Muslim.” Well, you don’t know that, because that isn’t the basis on which you’re judged — you can’t drill down into your own intention and sincerity to be quite sure.

The other benefit is that it enables us to give other people the benefit of the doubt. As we saw last time, even people who don’t seem to be doing so much, if they have a strong niyya, Allah can raise them to a very high degree, and we don’t know what people’s niyyas are — nobody can tell. Perhaps a truly perceptive spiritual guide can intuit certain things about people’s interior lives from body language, from the way they respond, from other, more mysterious forms of firasa. But generally it’s hard; it’s something hidden within us. So much human deceit comes about through people concealing their true intentions, and so much human suffering comes from that. We can’t tell. So if I see somebody who leaves the mosque after praying his fard, does two raka’at, and leaves, I don’t have the right to despise that person, because I know it’s the quality of those prayers that matters more. The quality is not just a multiplier — it gives his prayer its reality. So do not judge by appearances.

One of them used to strike himself and say, “Oh myself, my nafs, be sincere, be saved.” A sincere person, he said, is somebody who hides his good deeds just as he hides his bad ones — we all know we don’t like to talk about the bad things we’ve done, we hide them; a sincere person hides his good deeds just as much. Another used to say, “Blessed is he who takes one sound step wishing by it only the pleasure of Allah.” One of them wrote to a brother of his, saying, “Make sure your actions are completely pure in their intention, and even a little bit of action will benefit you” — for bringing about a pure intention in our actions is harder for people than the actions themselves. Whoever is pure, life will be pure for him; and whoever has a range of intentions, including bad ones, life will give him all kinds of difficulties.

This is another aspect of what we saw in the previous chapter: those who wait, as it were, for the fragrant cloud of a good niyya to come, so that the niyya truly corresponds with the action and the action has reality. The baraka of such people, even when what they’re doing doesn’t seem to be what we’d expect, brings incredible blessings through that synergy of niyya and action. Those people themselves represent a blessing, and that is the meaning of the awliya: those whose intentions and actions are in complete harmony, and who benefit others in ways we certainly can’t imagine.

One of the great saints of Baghdad used to say: it is better for you to pray two raka’at privately with complete sincerity than to write seventy — or seven hundred — hadiths with a good isnad; it will be more spiritually beneficial for you, because of that ikhlas. In sincerity for an hour there is salvation for eternity. But sincerity is rare. Knowledge is a seed, action is like sowing the seed, and ikhlas waters the seed. What Allah wants from human action is only the sincerity of the action. Allah has some servants who have understanding, and when they had understanding they did good deeds, and when they did those deeds they were sincere, and that sincerity summoned them to all the gates of goodness.

Everything can be reduced to two principles: God’s action through you, and your action for Him. Everything is ultimately the act of God through you, but at the same time — and this is one of the mysteries — it consists of actions that you do for Him; that is the fundamental, paradoxical basis of everything. In the first case you are content with what He has decreed, and you act for Him sincerely. If you are happy with both of these things, you will be successful in both worlds: you accept God’s decree, and you make your intention pure for Him. Those two things are the basis of iman, of this religion.

That is the end of his first section, in which he has talked about ikhlas and given us sayings explaining why it is meritorious, drawing particularly on the scriptures to show its centrality.

Defining Ikhlas: Purity and Singularity of Purpose▶ 24:54

He now moves on to an exposition of what ikhlas is. As always, he begins with the Qur’an, then the hadith, then the sayings of the early Muslims and the Sufis, which lend the discussion a certain fragrance and authority, showing that this is indeed a major concern of religion — and then he deepens our understanding through his own analysis. So: an exposition of the true nature of sincerity. We’ve talked about its merit, but what is it?

Know that everything can be imagined as susceptible to being corrupted by something else. When it is pure of those pollutants, and safe from them, it is described as being pure. This purified, protected action is known as ikhlas, which we translate as sincerity — an approximation, as usual with these Arabic, Qur’anic terms. The word khalisan has to do with uncontaminated purity in Arabic; “sincere” covers a slightly different lexical range, but we’ll stick with it. Allah has said that He takes out, from between filth and blood, milk that is pure, palatable to those who drink it. The purity of the milk lies in its being unpolluted by blood or excrement, or by anything else it might be mixed with. So we have this idea of ikhlas as uncontamination.

The opposite of ikhlas is shirk: impartnering — incorporating other things. It’s usually translated as idolatry, but “impartnering,” even if it’s a strange English word, gives a more literal translation, except that this impartnering comes in different degrees. Sincerity is the opposite of shirk in determining divinity, and shirk contains both what is hidden and what is manifest; ikhlas is similar, because the heart is constantly subject to alternating waves of sincerity and insincerity. Its habitat is the heart, and it pertains only to purposes and intentions.

We’ve already discussed the true nature of intention: niyya is all about a response to certain factors that promote it. To the extent that the factor is singular, the action that proceeds from that motivation is called ikhlas with respect to what is intended. So that’s the relationship between ikhlas and niyya — these factors, these reasons why we intend to do certain things: to the extent that we have a single reason, that is called ikhlas; it isn’t contaminated or mixed with anything else. So ikhlas carries the sense of singularity of purpose.

Somebody who gives something in charity purely to show off to others is, in this technical sense, sincere — because there is just one reason behind his intention, not a raft of different purposes. And whoever’s intention in giving is purely to draw close to his Creator is also, in this sense, sincere. So you could say the person doing something good for a bad reason is also, technically, sincere. But the Imam is saying it is our custom to apply “sincerity” as a religious virtue to those whose intentions are shaped by a single good purpose.

Just as ilhad, which we usually translate as atheism, apostasy, or deviation, simply means “inclination” — you could, on that basis, have a good ilhad — but generally in our terminology it refers to an inclination away from the truth. He is being quite linguistic and technical here in defining what he means by ikhlas, and this is very typical of his method.

Whoever’s purpose is simply to show off is exposing himself to destruction — but we’re not talking about that here. We’ve already discussed the question of ostentation, riya, in a special book, the Book of the Faults of Ostentation, elsewhere in the Ihya. The least we need to say about it here is that the texts indicate the ostentatious person — one who does good things for bad reasons — will be called by names such as show-off, deceiver, and polytheist on the Last Day.

When a Good Motive Is Mixed with a Worldly One▶ 33:03

What we’re going to talk about now is the ikhlas that concerns the singular purpose behind a niyya directed towards drawing close to the Creator — but where, alongside this motive, another motive has been mingled, whether ostentation or some other impurity of the self. So he isn’t going to talk about pure vices, people doing things for completely insincere reasons; he’s going to look at the question of degree of sincerity. That’s usually our experience — it’s quite unusual for people to do a religiously valid thing for a completely polluted reason, unless they’re merely pretending to be Muslim, and that’s unusual. Usually our problem is that the purity, the ikhlas, of our intention is contaminated, to some percentage, by something else. So it isn’t a single, pure motive that produces our niyya, but a complex, contaminated mesh of different purposes. That’s what he’s going to talk about in this chapter.

An example of something completely invalid would be somebody fasting purely to benefit medically from the fast. But you could have a mixed case at the same time, and he gives us a long list of examples of how our intention becomes mingled with our own worldly benefit:

  • Fasting to please Allah, but also hoping to get healthier as a result.
  • Freeing a slave in order to free himself from the expense that slave represented, and his bad manners.
  • Making the Hajj in order to get some exercise through the journeying.
  • Making the Hajj to escape some disturbance that has broken out in his own country, or to get away from an enemy in his household — some family dispute, some problem with his children — wanting to get away from that for a few days.
  • Going off to war in order to gain military experience, to learn about war, and to learn how to get a job as a great general leading armies.
  • Praying at night in order to stay awake, because he’s guarding his family or his riding animals on a journey — or, nowadays, somebody who prays all night because he doesn’t want to miss a 9 a.m. flight and it will keep him awake.
  • Seeking sacred knowledge to make finding a lucrative job easier, to gain status in his group, or to make his wealth and property safer because people venerate him as a scholar.
  • Teaching and preaching because he doesn’t like staying quiet and really enjoys talking.
  • Serving the scholars or the Sufis to improve his status in their eyes and in the eyes of ordinary people, or to gain some worldly advantage.
  • Writing out the whole Qur’an simply to improve his handwriting.
  • Going on Hajj but choosing to walk for the extra reward of more footsteps, when really it is to save the money he would have had to pay for an animal to ride.
  • Performing ghusl to get cleaner or cooler, or to smell better.
  • Narrating hadith so as to become famous for having short isnads.
  • Staying the night in a mosque simply so as not to have to pay for a hotel.
  • Fasting because he can’t be bothered to cook, or because he wants to get work done without being distracted by eating.
  • Giving money to a beggar just to stop him bugging him — like people at charity dinners who raise their pledge just so the whole thing can be over.
  • Visiting the sick in the hope that they will visit him when he is sick.
  • Going to other people’s funerals so that, when his own family have funerals, people will come and attend, and there will be a good turnout.

It’s interesting that, even though the Imam is writing so many centuries ago, just about every one of these things still applies. When we talk about these tricks of the ego, nothing has changed — human beings have not changed. We might have cars instead of donkeys going to Hajj, but these challenges to our ikhlas remain, and I think we can recognise ourselves in many of these characterisations.

Degrees of Contamination: Three Kinds of Mixed Motive▶ 41:32

To the extent that a person’s inner motivation is that he wishes to be closer to Allah, but one of these worldly ideas has also occurred to him, so that because of this secondary intention the practice becomes easier for him, his action is not really considered sincere to that extent, and this form of idolatry creeps into it. This is referenced in a hadith in Sahih Muslim: “I am the One who has the least need of partners” — you could translate it that way. This idea of shirk as the opposite of ikhlas makes us jump a little. He isn’t saying that if you don’t have a pure intention in these things you’re no longer a Muslim, but that you have a kind of multiple motivation, and therefore there’s a serious problem: the value of what you do is diminished to the extent that the secondary, worldly motive occupies a percentage of what is driving you.

To sum this up: all the shares of the pleasures of the world bring an ease to the ego. To the extent that these form part of our reason for doing things, the purity of that thing will be polluted, and its reward reduced. Human beings are swimming in their own desires; it’s very unusual for one of our actions, or one of our times of worship, to be free of some share of the kinds of things we’ve described.

This is why it is said that whoever, in his life, experiences one moment, one footstep, of absolute, uncompromising ikhlas will be saved. It doesn’t mean that moment outweighs everything else, but it does mean that, through the blessing of that experience and the inner purity behind it, that person is transformed. How many of us have ever experienced that hundred-per-cent ikhlas action? This is because of how unusual real ikhlas is, and how difficult it is to purify our hearts from these pollutants.

So the pure person is the one who has no motivation except the search for nearness to his Lord. If these worldly motivations are the only motivations someone has, then it’s obvious that person is in dire straits. But what we’re discussing in this section is only the case of the person whose basic intention is to draw close to Allah, but which is polluted to some extent by these other issues. These defects are either of the type of correspondence, or of impartnering, or of support, as we expressed already in the Book of Niyya — if you remember, a couple of lessons ago we discussed the three different factors involved in the generation of a niyya; it was a technical discussion.

To sum up: either the ego’s motive is equal to the religious motive, or it is stronger than it, or it is weaker than it. These are the three circumstances we can be in, and each has its own ruling, which we’re going to expound. But the aim is to purify one’s action from all of these pollutants — we must aspire to get rid of many of them, and even of the few that remain, until the ultimate motivation we have is simply to get closer to Allah, with no other motivation. That is when the pure intention for our actions will arise naturally.

Love of Allah as the Root of Sincerity▶ 48:26

You couldn’t imagine this happening, he says, except in the case of somebody who really loves Allah, who is intoxicated with love for Him, whose concern is wholly with the other world, the world of reality, so that there is no longer any settled place in his heart for love of worldly things and their worldliness. This is the target, and he also discusses it in the Kitab al-Mahabba, the Book of Love, which he says is the highest point: if you have this real love for Allah, all these other things, like ikhlas, flow naturally.

So how do we deal with the calamities of our age, which seem overwhelming? This is an age of multiple overwhelmings — climate change, artificial intelligence, Palestine, and the list is depressingly long. How do we cope with that? The scholars say: love for Allah. Don’t despair; still love Allah. If you have that love, the other things become easy, or at least bearable. He talks about this in the Kitab al-Mahabba.

So he’s now describing this person who is truly a lover of God. Such a person, when he eats or drinks, or even answers the call of nature, is pure in his action and sound in his intention, in every movement and every moment of stillness in his life. If he goes to sleep in order to be stronger for when he wakes to continue his life of worship, even his sleep is worship, and in his sleep he has the degree of the people of ikhlas. For somebody who doesn’t have this strong love of Allah, that door is generally closed, except in rare cases.

The person dominated by love of Allah and of the true, next world finds that his movements — even his ordinary, customary movements — acquire a kind of light, an illumination, from his underlying purpose, which is to draw nearer to his Lord. As for the person dominated by love of this world, of status and power — that which is not Allah — everything he does, even ordinary, profane things like making a cup of tea or sitting down, is coloured by that quality, because that is the basic motivation he has in life.

Therefore, the medicine that brings about sincerity consists in breaking the ego’s desires, ceasing to have grand aspirations for worldly things, and directing oneself seriously towards the next world, so that this becomes the dominant mood of the heart — and at that point, ikhlas becomes easy. So it’s about love; it’s about your fundamental orientation as a human being. Are you just about these tokens of esteem — letters after your name, numbers in your bank balance, envious glances as you drive by? Is that really what brings you pleasure in the world, or what you think brings you pleasure? Or do you instead have this unifying, single purpose, which is to draw near to the source of your being, your final destination, the ground of your being, al-Haqq, Reality? Do you choose batil, or do you choose haqq? Is it your nafs that is driving you, or is it the ruh that is active? If it’s the latter, then ikhlas is your natural state — again, it isn’t something like niyya that you can fabricate within yourself through an effort of will. It comes naturally from the deepest state, which in this case is love of Allah and love of reality.

The Hidden Fault: A Story of Wounded Pride▶ 54:22

This is a problem. We have so many actions in which we exhaust ourselves thinking they are purely done for Allah’s sake, but we are nonetheless beguiled and misled in this conviction, because we don’t know where the fault in our intention lies.

There’s a story about a man who prayed his five daily prayers in the first row of the mosque for thirty years without missing one. Then, one day, he had a strong reason for being slightly late to the mosque, and he prayed in the second row. He said: “A kind of embarrassment descended upon my heart, because people saw me now in the second row, and then I knew that people looking at me in the first row had brought me happiness, and had been the reason for a certain calmness in my soul that I wasn’t aware of. I didn’t know, for all those years, that I had been enjoying that — it was only when I didn’t get it that I realised it was there.”

These are the subtle aspects of the ego, and al-Ghazali is saying it’s really difficult for us to identify these pollutants in our intention, but clearly of gigantic importance. If we can even improve our ikhlas by ten per cent in our prayers — and we say so many prayers — that is a major breakthrough. This is why he’s stressing this; this is why the early Muslims stressed it; this is why it’s fundamental to the shape of our religious life.

This knowing that one is not sincere is an obscure and subtle matter. It’s unusual for actions to be really free of it, and unusual for people to be aware of these faults, except those given access to that awareness by Allah. Those who are ignorant of it think that their good deeds will all be accepted; and there appears to them from Allah what they had not reckoned on, and there appears to them the ugliness of what they had been doing.

The Scholar’s Temptation and the Devil’s Psychological Trick▶ 58:15

“Shall I tell you who are the greatest losers in their works? It is those whose actions are misguided in this world, while they think they are doing well.” The people most dangerously exposed to this fitna are the scholars — because the motive for most of them, when spreading knowledge, is the delight of being in a position of authority, taking pleasure in having followers, and being happy when people praise them. Shaytan deceives them through this and makes it hard for them to see it.

Here he’s getting quite close to the essence of the story of his own life. If you know his story, or have seen the film The Alchemist of Happiness, you’ll recall his catastrophic crisis of doubt about his own ikhlas — that perhaps he, with his hundreds of great students, as the greatest professor in the whole of the Dar al-Islam, was simply making a path for himself to Hell, because he wasn’t sincere, and people didn’t realise it. He had his famous crisis, or breakdown, and had to go off for ten years to recover his ikhlas and his confidence.

So he’s saying that most scholars are liable to this. They like positions of power; they like to be called doctor and professor; they like to give honorary, distinguished lectures. All of this — these ego things — is particularly significant in academia. Titles proliferate there more than perhaps in the aristocracy, and academics like to dress up more than anybody else, except perhaps some of the lawyers. So, again, this hasn’t changed. But Shaytan deceives scholars about their intentions, and this is a great psychological insight.

Your intention, you tell yourself, is just to spread Allah’s religion, to fight a battle on behalf of the law the Prophet laid down — so thinks the preacher giving advice to ordinary people and preaching to the powerful. He’s really happy that people are accepting his advice and crowding around him, and he thinks they’re happy because he has been upholding the religion. But should it happen that one of his peers preaches better than him, and everybody goes off to listen to the other, he is really upset and saddened. If his motive in all of this had been religion, he would give thanks to Allah, because somebody else is doing this task for him. And even then, Shaytan does not leave him alone: Shaytan says, “Yes, you’re unhappy because everybody’s going to the mosque down the road and not listening to you, but the reason you’re unhappy is that you’re not getting the reward — you’re not unhappy because people are no longer looking at you and are looking at somebody else instead.”

This is a kind of psychological wrinkle: when we feel, “I shouldn’t really be sad that somebody else is more popular,” Shaytan comes and whispers to us that the reason we’re sad is just that we’re not getting the reward any longer — which makes it feel better — because had those people stayed and been improved by what we were saying, we would have got the reward for all of that; and it’s good that we’re sad, because we’re sad about losing the reward. This poor scholar doesn’t realise, when he listens to this excuse, that his submission to Allah’s decree — accepting that somebody else is better, and handing the matter to somebody better than him — will bring him more reward than the reward that could have come had all those people stayed, listening to him and saying mashallah.

So this is Imam al-Ghazali again pushing back against this psychological defect in the mindset and the life of intention of scholars.

The Test of Umar and Abu Bakr▶ 1:04:59

“If only I could figure it out,” he says: had Umar, may Allah be pleased with him, been upset when Abu Bakr came first as imam, would his sadness have been praiseworthy or something to be condemned? It’s pretty obvious, but the whole point is that Umar was not saddened by that. And obviously, had Umar been sad that Abu Bakr became khalifa first and led the people in prayer in the mosque, even during the Prophet’s own lifetime, during his final sickness, that would have been reprehensible. Because his submission to Allah and His decree, and his handing over the matter to somebody better than him, would have been better for Umar in his religion than had he himself gained the reward of looking after the people’s affairs — even though doing that would have brought about very considerable reward. But in fact, the reality was that Umar was really happy to see somebody he knew was better than himself take over that position of authority. So what’s wrong with someone else also being happy in such a situation?

This is his psychological test: you think you’re doing things for Allah, you think all this reward is coming to you, and then somebody opens a slightly bigger masjid down the road, and its imam is a little better, has memorised more poetry than you have, and people start going there — or somebody is getting more hits on YouTube than your bayans are getting, and you’re unhappy about that. That is a sign of a lack of ikhlas. You should be happy that somebody else is doing the work of God, rather than thinking that your own glory, or possibly your income, is being threatened. Of course, the rivalry between mosques in Britain, and in very many countries, is often to do with status and ego, and it’s quite an ugly thing. We should be happy if somebody opens another mosque, but instead it’s often strange — a dar al-uloom opened simply to annoy another dar al-uloom. This is exactly the kind of defect of the ulama that Imam al-Ghazali is talking about.

So, to get to the end of this bayan: the tricks played by Shaytan on the ego of the scholar may well deceive some scholars. Applying this test to himself, a scholar might convince himself that, were somebody better than him to appear, of course he’d be happy about that. But telling ourselves something like that without actually being tested is just ignorance and pride. If we really think, “I’m so sincere that I wouldn’t mind if somebody else was doing these amazing things, and if somebody got more hits on YouTube than I am, I’d be so happy” — this, again, is one of the tricks Shaytan plays on us, because the ego is very willing to submit to this kind of conviction before such a circumstance actually arises. But when the misfortune comes, he changes, finds it awkward after all, and doesn’t carry out the promise he made. This kind of thing can only be understood by people who are really expert in the tricks of Shaytan and the ego, who have spent a long time examining and assessing the way the ego works.

Concluding Reflections▶ 1:09:53

So, to conclude: understanding the true nature of sincerity, and of acting sincerely, is a deep ocean in which just about everybody drowns, except the very unusual, exceptional individual — except for those of His servants who are purified in their sincerity. So let every servant be assiduous in inspecting and scrutinising these inner subtleties, otherwise he will become one of the followers of the devils without realising it.

This has again been quite an intense section, and we have to close at this point, although we haven’t finished the Book of Ikhlas yet — we’re going to get some anecdotes and sayings of the spiritual guides of Islam, of the great scholars, about ikhlas. But the Imam has given us what I think is a painful but quite realistic lesson about our insincerities: reminding us first of the great merit of being sincere, then explaining, in a kind of grammatical, logical, psychoanalytic way, what ikhlas actually is, and then giving us examples, focusing particularly on the problem of his colleagues and his former self, the scholars — how dangerous it is when the leaders of religion are really interested in the natural veneration ordinary people have for them. It’s extraordinarily dangerous, something he returns to elsewhere in the Ihya, but these passages have always been read with considerable sobriety and attention by the ulama, because an alim has to work hard to reach his degree of knowledge, and his role is an exhausting one. To spend all of one’s time and treasure acquiring that knowledge, and for it still not to be quite right, because one is still as much a worldling as the man selling shawarma outside who just wants to be popular and to have people queue up outside his shop, is a major problem. The ulama have always been aware of this odium theologicum.

One of the reasons Imam al-Ghazali is writing this is really for the common people, to explain to them that religion is not just about outward forms — teaching outward forms, preaching outward forms — but about an inner reality, a light within, and that the purpose should simply be to come close to the divine reality and to love God.

So, alhamdulillah, we’ve come to the end of that section, and inshallah the fifth lecture will follow, when we will be able to conclude this second of the three sections of the Book of Intention. May Allah subhana wa ta’ala grant us the blessing of ikhlas, give us the delight of sincerely approaching Him, judge us on the basis of our most sincere and best actions, and overlook the impurities of our purpose.
