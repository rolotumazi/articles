The Pathology Lab in the Basement▶ 55:36

The nurse who had organized the in-service was crying before Najah Bazzy finished speaking. Her name was Teresa, she was Christian, she wore a cross, and she had set up the very class on the care of Muslim mothers and the dead that Bazzy was teaching. When the room emptied, Teresa said only, “Follow me.” They rode the elevator down. One door opened, then another, into an empty pathology lab. Teresa pointed and broke down, sobbing too hard to speak. On two shelves stood rows of brown jugs, each with a green disposition paper taped to it.

Bazzy asked if she could pull one down. With gloves on, she opened it and a fetus came into her hand. She looked in the jar and there was another, and another — three in one container, in jars and tubs preserved in formaldehyde dating back to 1995, 1996, 1997. She realized they were all babies, perhaps two hundred of them. “I wrote a big article about it in our youth newspaper,” she recalls, called Babies Stored in Jars and Tubs — she still has the clipping.

The babies could not be moved because no one had signed the green disposition form that legally releases such remains. Bazzy assembled the hospital’s highest authorities, who had no idea what sat in their own basement. She was permitted to bury exactly one infant, a boy named Ahmed, whose name she found on the list. All the rest were cremated. The wound did not close. Some time later, lecturing to a few hundred people at Henry Ford Community College, she told the story; afterward a smiling woman approached her and said, “I’m the one who cremated all of those babies that you saved.” Bazzy went numb. The woman had worked for a funeral home with a crematory and believed she had done something kind — the babies were no longer in jars. For Muslims, cremation is a desecration. Out of that horror Bazzy founded a second program, Plots for Tots, dedicated to infant burial.

How the Babies Came to Be Discarded▶ 40:57

The chain of events began earlier, with the Iraqi refugees arriving in Dearborn in large numbers after the Gulf War. Many came gravely ill from refugee camps — undescribable cancers, anomalies, infants born with strange deformities. By then Bazzy was no longer a bedside nurse but a clinical transcultural nurse with a subspecialty in medical ethics, solving ethical problems and writing policy for Muslim health care; at the time, she says, there were only thirty-three transcultural nurses in the world and none of them clinical.

The reckoning came at an Ashura gathering at the Joy Road Mosque. A woman approached and asked, “Did you bury my baby?” The hospital had told her so, the woman said, and the baby was coming to her in dreams, crying, asking to be visited. Bazzy took her name, pulled the hospital record, and discovered the baby had been cremated. She was furious. Investigating, she learned the cause: a single document, the green disposition form, signed at the time of a fetal demise. Many Iraqi refugees had no idea what they were signing because no one translated it. Medicaid paid only seventy-five dollars toward an infant’s disposition — enough to cremate, not to bury. Misexplained, the forms sent these babies to a research pathology lab at the University of Michigan, where they became wards of the state and were collectively, ceremonially cremated every six months.

Bazzy convened the highest authorities, had the forms translated, and built a department of medically trained translators so that anyone who did not speak English — not only Muslims — would understand what they were signing. She once chased a hearse on the way to Ann Arbor to recapture a baby. And she began burying infants herself, asking doctors for five hundred dollars at a time — “Of course, no one said no.” The Quranic principle behind the work, she says, is the hadith she first learned in English as a young woman: if you see an injustice, fix it with your hand; if you cannot, speak against it with your tongue; if you cannot, condemn it in your heart, and that is the weakest of faith. “I can’t turn away from these things. I cannot. I will not. Like who would I be if I were to turn away?”

The Baby in the Laundry Basket

The first burial she ever promised grew out of another case that opened her to this work. An Iraqi couple had twins; the first died, and the second — blind at birth, intestines outside the belly, on a ventilator — was never going to survive. The physician and, eventually, the neonatal intensivist herself wanted to remove the ventilator, but the family followed Sayyid Sistani and could not consent. The ethics team, on which Bazzy normally sat, barred her from the case for being too close. The hospital had the legal right to remove the ventilator anyway. She refused to accept it, both for the family’s sake and because the Iraqi community’s anger at its treatment was becoming a crisis.

Using the three principles of her transcultural model — how to accommodate, how to negotiate, how to repattern — she got the baby home off the ventilator with a few days of hospice support, which she secured by calling a hospice director friend even though hospice does not normally support ventilated infants. Visiting the family, she found a house she assumed they were just moving into; in fact it was bare because that was all they had. The baby’s formula sat in a Styrofoam cooler on ice. The infant was presented to her in a white laundry basket, lying on new, neatly folded towels with the tags still on, the parents calm and full of grace. What struck her most, and still moves her to tears, was the dignity in their eyes. She promised to bury the baby, then sat in her minivan and called her mother — who was moving house and said, “Come, take whatever you want.” Bazzy rented a U-Haul and, with her four children, outfitted the family’s entire home within a day. The baby died a few days later, and she kept her promise.

A Servant Before Any Public Label▶ 2:07

Asked who she is beneath the public labels — founder of Zaman International, CNN Hero, mother — Bazzy answers without hesitation: a servant, then a mother, wife, daughter, friend. She does not see the public titles at all. The servanthood, she insists, is not aspiration or extra credit but a basic responsibility: humans were created to take care of one another, and that is the covenant taken with God. Feeding the poor, she says, is simply the right thing to do, not something inspirational. She even resists the word “founder” applied to Zaman — “Allah is the founder of all things. We are the servants of the missions placed in front of us. I didn’t give myself a brain. I didn’t give myself a heart.” Her prayer, she says, is only that God use her as one of his vessels, as much as she can be used.

That posture crystallized at Hajj, beneath the golden waterspout of the Kaaba, the Mizab ar-Rahmah, where she had a “spiritual synapse.” Scholars tell believers to ask God for what they need, and she had been doing exactly that — until, in a split second, she changed her supplication to ask not what she wanted from God but what God wanted from her: “Make me what you want me to be, not what I want to be.” If she pursued her own vision she might fall; pursuing his, she reasoned, her chances were far better. That mindset, she says, fills your container; once God sees it filled with what he intended, the lid comes off and more capacity flows in — a spiritual maturity. It also let her release her once color-coded executive schedule entirely to God, which paradoxically freed her to say no to far more.

A Sushi Household and the End of Racism▶ 17:50

Bazzy’s family has been in America for more than 135 years; her grandfather immigrated from Tibnin in southern Lebanon in 1885, among the first such families. The women crossed oceans by boat, bore children here, returned to raise them, and brought them back as adults. Her father was born in the United States, raised in Lebanon, and came back through Palestine at fourteen, never to leave again. Her mother, from Syria, was raised in a Christian-Muslim village before a love story unfolded in a two-family flat: her father’s family rented the upstairs, he saw her mother hanging laundry, and fell head over heels. They married despite the Sunni-Shia divide — her father Shia, her mother Sunni — making theirs what Bazzy calls a “sushi” household.

Her mother’s open-mindedness was extraordinary for its time. On the Sunni-Shia question she would say, “We believe in the same God. We read the same Quran. We go to the same Hajj. We fast Ramadan the same way. We pray the same. In this house, we don’t have that issue.” That broadened Bazzy’s sense that Islam had wider borders than many families allowed. As an adult she came to see the sectarian dialect as deeply damaging — the people hurting Muslims most, she notes, do not care which side anyone is on, and God’s mercy, Ar-Rahman Ar-Raheem, encompasses every particle of the universe. She holds that it is hers to decide her own theology and get herself and her children to heaven, but not to judge the theology of others.

The deepest lesson came at seven, during the 1967 race riots, when she watched her beloved aunt — daughter of the community’s first imam — scoop up a Black baby and cover it with kisses, showing total love, at a time when she was being taught to lock the doors and Dearborn itself was white and racist. “She gave me permission to enter the world of all of us,” Bazzy says. Whatever racism might have taken root was erased from her heart in that moment — proof, she says, of the power of an adult showing children how to be.

The Mother She Brags About, and a Father’s Love

Bazzy speaks of her mother, ninety-four and illiterate but, she insists, brilliant — “if she had the opportunity to go to school, you’d be in the White House.” When Bazzy taped the 206 bones of the human body to her wall to study them, her mother studied the chart too, seeing the opportunity she herself never had, and became her biggest supporter. Strict and fair, the mother never defended her children when they were wrong; what is broken in parenting today, she now says, is parents siding with their kids against teachers, something that would never have happened in her day.

Her parents modeled a partnership Bazzy believes the culture has lost. Her father was firm in his love and showed it; her mother was “the queen.” He was calm and the negotiator, she the disciplinarian, and they never undercut each other — “If you want to negotiate a deal, you do it with Baba. And stay out of trouble with mom.” They joked, hugged, went on dates, fought like any couple, but operated as a team: not one hand over the other but a handshake. A girl raised with a father who loves her mother that way, Bazzy says, grows up looking for that same stability in a spouse — as she did. She offers the perhaps old-fashioned view that boys draw their sense of self early from their mothers and girls from their fathers. The widespread idea that a stable household requires a man to be firm, distant, and barely present she rejects flatly: their faith never commanded it, it makes no sense, “but for some reason culture has us in this chokehold.”

Wanting to Be a Doctor in a World That Wouldn’t Allow It

Bazzy never wanted to be a nurse — she wanted to be a doctor, with what she calls a clinical brain the whole family relied on for medical advice. Part of that came from her brother Sammy, who had muscular dystrophy and could not move a day in his life; by the time she was a teenager she was bathing, grooming, and tending to all his needs, so medicine felt natural. But the culture of her day in the south end of Dearborn did not let Arab girls pursue school; most were engaged by sixteen, and her mother’s system was to find spouses for her children in Lebanon or Syria. Her mother feared that sending her to Wayne State, in what was then called the murder capital of the nation, would get her killed, and put her foot down against medical school.

Bazzy refused to follow the marriage track — she wanted to fall in love and bristled at the courtship rituals of making coffee for suitors, thinking, “I’m not on display here,” even as she understood her mother’s and grandmother’s hearts. Her father negotiated the compromise: Henry Ford Community College, five minutes away, with one of the country’s best two-year clinical nursing programs. Her mother went from opponent to her biggest fan. Throughout, Bazzy kept every household responsibility — she was, in the Syrian tradition of adab, taught to keep a spotless home — pulling all-nighters while cooking, cleaning, and caring for Sammy. She did not finish her bachelor’s degree until thirty-six, raising four children alongside her studies, their A papers on the refrigerator together. Her advice to anyone delaying education for family: “You’re going to get there with it or without it. So you may as well take your time and get there with it.”

Finding Your God-Given Gift▶ 1:12:54

A recurring question among younger people, the conversation turns, is “What is my purpose? I don’t know what I’m meant to do in life.” Bazzy teaches a leadership exercise drawn from the StrengthsFinder assessment — a Gallup-backed study of leaders that segments a person’s natural DNA talents — and requires everyone who works with her at Zaman or the youth group to take it, because she needs to know “how did God make you.” But the simplest test, she says, is memory: there are things you have done your whole life and never tire of doing.

She demonstrates it live on the host, walking him back through childhood — toys, Pokémon and Yu-Gi-Oh cards, sports video games — until a thread emerges: as a boy he collected cards for their individual strengths and attached a story to every action figure before he could play. “What you’re doing now is collecting stories and strength,” she tells him. “You’re still doing that. You’re doing it through your podcast. That talent never leaves. It just evolves.” She ran the same test on herself: her mother confirmed the only toy she ever wanted was a plastic medical kit, that she gave her younger brother shots, that her father’s green canvas army surgical kit — its smell she can still recall — made her heart pound. She became a surgical, open-heart nurse; now she is not in an operating room but, as she puts it, “still stitching the world up, still pumping a heart, still feeding people. The talent never leaves. It just gets rerouted.”

God, she argues, is “an active, purposeful creator” who gave every person a distinct skill set and made it easy, not hard, to reach him — “we just have to plug in.” The reason gifts feel so hard to find is partly upbringing and discipline. She credits her own ordered childhood, where everything had a method: how to enter the house, wash your hands, set the table, make your bed. She invokes the admiral’s bestseller Make Your Bed — a single small mastered task that anchors the whole day — and notes her mother knew this without ever being a four-star admiral. Parents today, she says, often lack the time to spot and nurture a child’s traits, which are visible by about eighteen months, because someone else is raising the child. People have abandoned the kitchen, where, she insists, family life and nearly every life skill — math, creativity, cleanliness, access to food — are actually learned.

Putting the Home First, Out Loud▶ 1:27:21

Now in her sixties, Bazzy says she is “brave enough” to say what triggers people: God first, then family — her children, parents, siblings, in-laws — and only then everything else. “God isn’t going to ask me what kind of executive I was. It’s going to ask me what kind of mother I was. What kind of wife. What kind of daughter.” People assume someone with her public work cannot possibly do what other mothers do; she finds the perception hilarious and answers, “How do you know I don’t have time?” The premise of her warning is the wreckage she sees — a roughly 60 percent divorce rate, “marry today, divorce tomorrow,” families falling apart, privileged children, demeaned teachers. Much of it she traces to having too much, too fast: when wealth (which is not forbidden) comes quickly rather than step by step, it is hard to appreciate, and instant gratification bleeds into relationships where people treat marriage as one option among many.

She is not against women’s education or economic independence — she lived through the feminist movement, fought for a place when women were barely admitted to medical school, and celebrates that med-school classes are now evenly split. But she tells women who simply want to stay home that wanting only that “cuts yourself short of the divine plan,” because humans were made for balance: educated and caring for the home. Her central image is a family as a baseball team — first, second, third base, and a bench. Not everyone can be at bat at once; in a healthy family each person, including the mother, gets a turn to run the bases while the others sit on the bench in support. She herself batted when the children supported her education; she sat the bench while her husband traveled the world for work. Trying to have everyone bat at the same time is impossible, and it is why so many are exhausted. She applauds the women now scaling back to part-time as reclaiming ownership of motherhood, spousehood, and their own careers — because in the end, she says, “not a person in corporate America is going to read you the Fatiha or come to your grave. It’s your children.”

Before couples reach that point, Bazzy presses young people toward conversations she says have stopped happening — about money, conflict, cleanliness, and core values, because newlyweds are living with someone they did not grow up with. But the question she insists matters most is whether a partner can get your children to heaven, and whether, if you died or were incapacitated, that spouse could raise your children the way you would want. If yes, that is the right person. “Please, please,” she urges, “have these very difficult conversations with the people that you’re sitting to have coffee with.”

Choosing Islam by Intellect▶ 1:37:08

Raised between a fearful version of God — a grandmother pointing at a factory flame and warning that Allah would burn her for wearing shorts — and “haram or hype” as the only two categories, Bazzy nonetheless felt close to God as a child, talking to him from the swings at six or seven. Those two stark options, she says, drove her to study world religions and decide for herself. Christianity’s God of total forgiveness did not draw her; the theology made little sense to her, however much she respects others’ choices. What did move her was the Quranic account of Mary, turned out by her tribe, and the infant Jesus defending his mother from the cradle — a story found only in Islam. The image still makes her heart flutter, and after 9/11, when too few Muslim women were available to speak, she found herself going church to church telling it, weeping each time, and watching the Christian audiences weep too. “Upon hearing the truth, they will shed tears,” she says, quoting the Quran. She calls herself “an intellectual convert to Islam,” to the Ja’fari school, and still studies with her teacher, Sayyid Muhammad Baqir.

An Allergy to Injustice, and Whom Mercy Is For

Bazzy traces her acute sense of injustice to early childhood. At three and a half she watched her military father — a man she had never seen cry — sob through President Kennedy’s funeral, and saw John Jr. salute the casket; it was her first experience of death and, she thinks, of injustice and the orphaned child. As a child of the 1960s she absorbed racism, segregation, Vietnam, yellow ribbons, the National Guard clubbing kids past curfew in a Dearborn park. The instinct never left: in a Beirut café she watched a woman slap her young Ethiopian maid across the face and had to fight the urge to slap the woman back, a regret that stays with her. In the emergency room she saw many women from her community arrive severely beaten — they needed to be divorced, she says, “because that’s not what God would have wanted for them.” Of all griefs, none reaches the depth she feels during Ashura, mourning the injustice of Imam Hussein, which she says comes from a different place in the soul.

This same conviction shapes Zaman International. When the host first met the women in its programs years ago, he was surprised to find them African American, Ukrainian, Latina, and Middle Eastern, having assumed Zaman served Middle Easterners. Bazzy’s reply: “It’s haram for it not to be like that.” The Quran commands care of women, children, the orphan, and the wayfarer without ever saying the Muslim one. “The highest form of faith is to care for the human family. And the highest form of racism is to take care of your own and only your own.” When donors offer money earmarked for one group only, she sometimes refuses it. As the Goodfellows once knocked on doors to bring Christmas gifts to poor Muslim children in the south end — children who, surrounded by love, never knew they were poor — she sees it as her duty to care for other people’s children in return.

Zaman, now thirty years old, did not begin as an institution. For its first decade it gave money, clothes, washers, dryers, food boxes, Eid and Christmas presents, coat drives — for fifteen years out of the back of her van, with no office and no address — until she left paid work to run it full time while caring for an ailing father and a sister with cancer. But the deeper shift was from giving money to giving capability: rather than handing the women cash, Zaman builds businesses, teaching them to make something with their hands and sell it. Her greatest passion, though, remains the youth. The leadership group she started in 1997 (now the MYC, in its twenty-ninth year) has run more than three hundred young people through its rotating leadership cohorts, many now in their forties with children of their own. Investing in them, she says, is investing in the future of Islam in America: when you lay your head in the ground, you must leave behind two things in good shape — your family and your community.

A Death Doula’s View of Dying

Washing the dead is not unusual to Bazzy; she has done it since she was fifteen, taught by the same aunt who kissed the Black baby. The aunt, named Khadijah, was carrying a bag to Toledo to wash a body and told the girl, “I’m going to teach you how to do it, because you’re going to wash me one day.” She never formally taught her, but Bazzy did wash her, learning from the old women of the community; she has since passed the practice on, and the mosques now have dedicated ghusl rooms and trained teams. She calls herself a death doula — as a birth doula assists at the entrance to life, she assists at the exit — and has helped many people in her community leave the earth. “It’s a very calming thing for me,” she says. “It’s a privilege.”

Across forty-plus years and regardless of religion, ethnicity, or gender, she says, the dying tend to say the same things and have similar experiences, much of which she keeps private, including secrets they entrust to her that go to her grave. There is a settling serenity, a sukoon and sakina; people speak of seeing lost loved ones and of the light; some need to make amends or send a final message. She frames it medically too: the lower brain stem fights for survival, but the higher brain — the intellect of the soul — kicks in and lets the body settle into peace, which she finds awesome to witness. The transition itself, she says without doubt, is beautiful.

She does not flinch from her own mortality. As a relentless event planner she has written out twelve pages of instructions, kept in a Google Doc on her son’s drive — step by step, even to who should carry her casket — because she cannot abide chaos and does not want her loved ones guessing or grieving more than necessary, and because she is aware that the more public a life becomes, the smaller and more precious its true core grows. One leadership exercise she gives the chaplains she trains at the University of Chicago, and gently to the youth, is to write your own obituary: if you were not here, what would you want said, and who would say it? It is hard precisely because it forces people to face their mortality.

The Last One

For all that knowledge, Bazzy is candid that grief still stings — “the more you love, the more it stings” — especially the most recent loss, an elder brother who died two months earlier. He had called and said, “I need my sister.” At the hospital he told her, “It’s time. I’m not going to be here tomorrow,” and she answered, “Okay, let’s get you ready, habibi. Whatever you need.” The siblings, children, and grandchildren never left his side. She stayed beside him, read him Quran, stroked his head, brought each person in to say goodbye. Hours before he died he woke and asked, “Naj, what’s taking so long?” She told him, “Allah is not ready for you, habibi. You have to be patient.” She has no conversations she wishes she had had — “I’m good at that. Making sure there’s no words that go unsaid.” She knows where her dead are and is happy for them; the hard part is the hole they leave.

Her brother Sammy also gave a measure of her own family. He never moved, yet lived to nearly seventy though doctors gave him a year, then seven, then thirteen at most. He tutored everyone’s children, ran a Muscular Dystrophy Association adult camp, and ordered the family’s pharmacy needs online — all by moving a mouse with his eyes. A thousand people came to his funeral. “If he can do that and not move, what am I supposed to do if I can move?” she asks. “Because he could not move, I did move.” Asked, in turn, whom she would call in her own last hours, she pauses: she does not think she would need anyone, because her husband and children are raised to know exactly what she needs, and would simply never leave. In closing she asks forgiveness of anyone her words may have stung, knowing they come from love and from lessons learned, and returns to her single measure of a life: focus on the people who will read your Fatiha and do good on your behalf when you are gone, “because that’s really what matters ultimately in the end.”
