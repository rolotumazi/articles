Welcome to Not From Here

Jafar: Najah, welcome to Not From Here. You’re probably one of the most requested episodes that I’ve ever gotten.

Najah: Really?

Jafar: Anytime I put up any type of poll of who you guys want to see, all I get is Najah Bazzy, Najah Bazzy, Najah Bazzy. So here you are. The way we start this show off usually is by you introducing yourself in the way that you see yourself. People know you as a lot of things — the founder of Zaman, a CNN hero, a mother. Najah: Yeah, I see myself as a servant before anything else.

And if we see ourselves as spiritual beings having a human experience, in that human experience is a lot of imperfection. That’s the beauty of it. Next, I would definitely see — and my husband may laugh if he was listening to this, he’d say, “Of course she sees herself as a mother first.” But certainly I do. And grandmother now.

A Moment in Hajj at the Kaaba

Jafar: I want to ask you, if you could pinpoint a moment where you feel like your story really started, where this version of Najah really came to be — what would that moment in time be?

Najah: There’s many. There isn’t a story. There are multiple stories that I’m writing about in a book, as you know, and I’m trying to talk about those stories in the book.

I think the starting point for me, though, at some point in time — even though it may not have been when I was a child, it certainly was when I was an older adult — was when I was in Hajj. I remember that pivotal moment. We ask Allah for things, we ask God for things, and we’re often told by scholars, “Ask God for what you need. Do your dua. Ask God for what you need.” One pivotal moment was at the Kaaba. I love going to Hajj. It was under the golden gutter, the Mizab ar-Rahmah, and there’s some sort of magnetic force, some sort of spiritual force on that particular wall. You see people just fall apart. And I was having that falling-apart moment. Imam Zayn al-Abidin came to my mind because he often prayed there.

I realized it was what I call a spiritual synapse. That’s what I named it, and that’s what I continue to call it. It was just after the moment where I realized I’m asking him what I need. What I should be asking him is what he wants from me, not what I want from him. So in a split second, I changed my dua to: make me what you want me to be, not what I want to be. Because if it’s what I want to be, I might certainly fall. If it’s what he wants me to be, my chances of getting there are going to be a whole lot better. But that mindset allows you to fill your container. And then when Allah subhana wa ta’ala knows you filled it with what he wants you to be, I feel like the lid can come off and more capacity can come in. And God can take you to the next level of what he wants you to be. So I kind of see it as a spiritual maturity.

But that was in that one moment. There were other really key moments, since I was a young child, that are very vivid and that changed something in me so deep that I could never go back.

The Aunt Who Erased Racism From Her Heart

Jafar: Did any of those have to do with your parents at all?

Najah: Of course. One had to do with my aunt. She was my halo’s wife and the daughter of Sheikh Khalil Bazzi, the first imam here. They lived in Detroit. Her home was open all of the time to all of these kids on the block. It was like what we used to call the Kool-Aid house on the block, because everybody would come over to her house.

One day in the mid-60s — 1967, to be exact, I was 7 years old — it was the race riots in Dearborn, Detroit. These are national race riots. The city of Detroit had gone through a horrendous time with the race riots. I remember watching her pick up a baby who was African American, a black baby, and start kissing this baby like crazy, just showing total love. I remember that so well, because I was then taught to close the doors, to lock the doors. You grow up in Dearborn. Dearborn was very white. Dearborn was very racist. And she gave me permission to enter the world of all of us.

Had I not seen that moment — because I loved her so much — I probably may have been different. But in that moment, if there was something called racism, it was erased from my heart at a very early age. That’s the power of an adult giving children examples of how to be. And that was a powerful example.

Of course, my parents, my grandmother — but my father is Shia, my mother is Sunni. I grew up in what I call a Sushi household. What my parents taught me, especially my mother, my father too — my father was very patriotic. He was an army man. He served in the military. My uncles served in the Marines. We had a lot of family members that served in the United States military, and the flag was a big deal for us. There was a sense of patriotism that my father and mother brought into our home. Respect for this country. They might not have agreed with everything, but there was certainly a sense of respect for religious freedom. They used to talk a lot about that.

But my mom’s open-mindedness to everything was extraordinary for her time. And still is. She’s extraordinary. And I think that open-mindedness led to a household of open-minded people, open-hearted people.

Jafar: Open-mindedness in the sense of accepting everybody? That type of open-mindedness?

Najah: No. Open-mindedness like, in the Sunni-Shia context, my mom would say, “I don’t understand what this issue is. We believe in the same God. We read the same Quran. We go to the same Hajj. We fast Ramadan the same way. We pray the same. In this house, we don’t have that issue.” And so I have a very mixed family. Even though I grew up in Dearborn, and I grew up in Shia centers my whole life, it was this idea that Islam had broader borders than other families may have grown up with. The expansion of our idea that Allah subhana wa ta’ala — my mom used to say this — if he created Judaism and Christianity, maybe Islam is also a variety of things. Of course, I studied on my own and I chose what was best for myself, but that was a personal journey that I went through. That was the kind of talk she would have.

But I was raised very culturally hodd. Very much so.

The Damage of the Shia-Sunni Divide

Allah subhana wa ta’ala — when we say Ar-Rahman Ar-Raheem — that encompasses all of us.” That was a big turning point in how I brought about the subject.

But in your case, being in a Sushi household, and seeing that moment with your aunt — those were like building blocks to the work that you do, because you can’t be in the space that you are if you look at things very black and white. You have to have that visual of: we’re all the same, we’re all part of one community at the end of the day.

Najah: I think it’s Allah’s command. He says that we are one family, we are one human family. And Imam Ali says it beautifully in his very famous saying: if you are not my brother in faith, you are my brother in humanity. But Allah’s rahman encompasses more than our human family. It encompasses every particle in the universe. And so it’s for me to decide what my theology is going to be. But it’s not for me — and I’m saying this personally — to judge the theology of others, especially Muslims. I have to get myself to heaven and my children. That’s my responsibility. How other people get there is their responsibility.

A Family in America for 135 Years

Jafar: How did your family get to America? Because your family has been here for a very long time.

Najah: Yeah, over 135 years.

Jafar: One of the first families, I believe.

Najah: First families.

Jafar: Amazing. What’s the story of them coming here?

Najah: My grandfather, actually my jido, immigrated in 1885 from Lebanon, from Tibnin, the south. He was first to arrive. Their story’s so beautiful and so cute. My jido came, and then back then — this is how amazing and how much strength these people had — they would come here, imagine these women coming in these boats across oceans, God only knows how many days. They would stay here a couple of years. They had some of their children here. They would go back, take these little children, raise them there, bring these children back. The children would come as adults. That was my father’s story. He was born here in the United States. He went back to Lebanon, was raised there, came back here through Palestine at 14 years of age. He never went back.

Mama was born here at Henry Ford Hospital here in Detroit, and was here till she was about three. Her mom took her back to Syria. My mom is from Syria. She was raised in Syria in front of some amazing castle in a Christian-Muslim village, which is another story about Mom. And then she came back here when she was 15.

The way they fell in love: my mother’s family owned the house. My father’s family was coming back a second or third time — I can’t remember — and they needed a place to live. It was a two-family flat. My father’s family rented the upstairs, saw my mother, you know, typical Arab girl. We say “I’m tensioning” — the clothes, the laundry, hanging them on the line. He fell head over heels in love with her. And they struggled to get married because of the Sunni-Shia issue. But they overcame that. And theirs was quite the love story.

A Father’s Love and a Household Built on Stability

Najah: I always say that if you want a good household — when a father, a man, loves the mother as much as my father loved my mother, and showed it and expressed it — the kids grow up with a very strong sense of stability.

Jafar: Why do you say that?

Najah: Because he was very firm about his love for her. She was the queen. It was that simple. She was the queen. We would listen. We would obey the queen. She was loving and tough at the same time. And they both agreed on the way that they would raise us. He was calm and quiet and the one who would listen, and Mom was more of the disciplinarian. They never separated that. So we knew as children — we’re six kids — that this is the way it was going to be. If you want to negotiate a deal, you do it with Baba. And stay out of trouble with Mom.

I just feel like, as a woman, when you are raised with a father that shows your mother that kind of love, as a girl growing up, that stability is what you look for in a spouse. And I certainly did.

Jafar: Do you think we’ve lost that?

Najah: Absolutely, Jafar. We’ve lost that.

Jafar: Although we think of the older generation as these strict men who didn’t show love at all, and we kind of emulate that in our own relationships because we think that’s how a man should be. You tell her what to do and leave the house, or whatever the case may be.

Najah: Yeah, their love was quite different. He loved his girls, and we knew that. I think that sense of womanhood, childhood — whether you’re a male or a female — this might sound a little old-fashioned, but I’m going to say it. I think boys get their sense of self from their moms, and girls get it from their dads early on. I think that’s a real thing. I really do. And of course, those relationships evolve, and as a young man is growing, he learns more from his father, or needs his father more in different ways. But I think a mom’s love to her children is paramount. And I think the way she sees her son helps him to grow to be a man. And the way that a father deals with his daughter helps her to be a woman and a great wife and a good mother. I attribute everything that I have or do to them, because they are wonderful. It’s not as though it was a cherry-pie household. It’s not that. But definitely there was love. There’s no doubt about that, and stability.

Jafar: Why do you think men are like this, then — where they think the way to a stable household is being firm and almost not being present?

Najah: I don’t know. Something’s missing in the equation. Something’s missing in their upbringing. It’s odd, because even our faith doesn’t tell us to do it. It doesn’t make sense to do it. But for some reason culture has us in this chokehold that says this is the only way. That is not the only way. In my opinion, it’s the wrong way. You know, it’s a shared value system.

My father and mother — they would laugh in front of us. They would joke. They would hug. On any occasion, they would do a little bit more. They would go out on dates together. They would get dressed up and go out, and we would watch them do that. There was a lot of pride in their marriage. Of course, they fought a lot, like most couples probably did back then, but they were a team. That’s the bottom line. It wasn’t one hand over the other. It was a handshake. Each had their strengths, each had their weaknesses, and they complemented each other beautifully. And here we are.

Jafar: And it’s carried on through the generations. Like you and your siblings — you see that pattern that they all looked for that same type of love that your parents had?

Najah: Absolutely. That same kind of stability, I would say.

Wanting to Be a Doctor, and Caring for Sammy

Jafar: I want to fast forward a little bit, to you going the medical route — you wanting to be a nurse. Does that come from someone suggesting, “Hey Najah, I think you’d be really good in the medical field”? Or does that come from something deeper than that?

Najah: Much deeper than that. I never wanted to be a nurse. I wanted to be a doctor. Very much so. I have a very clinical brain. I thank God for that clinical brain all of the time. It’s a joke in our family household, and even in the broader family, because prior to this next generation becoming physicians, almost everyone called me for medical advice. Innately, I understand medicine.

But I have a brother that could not walk, Ali Hamou, so I grew up with a brother that had muscular dystrophy. He could never move. Never moved. Not a day in his life. We grew up taking care of him. By the time you’re 5 or 6 years old, you’re moving his foot, you’re moving his arm, you’re giving him something, you’re helping him. By the time you’re 12 or 13, you’re grooming him. By the time you’re 15, you’re taking care of his basic human needs. You’re showering him, taking him to the bathroom, brushing his teeth, cleaning his ears, brushing his hair, getting him ready for school. You’re doing everything.

Jafar: He can’t move at all? Can he speak?

Najah: He could speak. And he was brilliant. So growing up with that, it seemed natural to me to be in medicine. But the culture of my day did not allow Arab girls to go to school. They were lucky if they finished high school.

Jafar: Even here in America?

Najah: Well, I grew up in the south end of Dearborn, so I’m not sure if that’s what we call here in America. In the south end of Dearborn — well, I’m a south ender. That’s a very special title for anyone listening to your show who’s grown up in the south end. We have a very special pride about ourselves. They say: south ender, always a south ender. It was a tough place to grow up, because our families were fierce. Fierce. But talk about a community — there’s nothing like it. Fierce about their kids. Fierce about their reputations. Fierce about their girls. Fierce about their households. These women were warriors. Literally. If people could be like them and have the fortitude they had, this community would be incredible. We stand on their shoulders.

Most of my family members, by 16, were engaged. Some finished high school and then immediately got married. Some did not finish high school. So here I come along. I couldn’t quite go along with Mom’s system. Mom’s system was: you’re 19, you’re a young man, ship you to Syria, you get married, or to Lebanon. You go someplace, find a girl from over there, and you get married. That was the system. So my brother got married young at 19, my eldest brother. My sister got married at 18 right after high school, from Syria. Sammy, of course — Ali Alhamou — did not get married. But that system wasn’t going to work for me.

Negotiating Her Way Into Nursing School

Najah: I wanted to go to medical school. It was unheard of. No way, no how. My father would have agreed, but the queen put her foot down and said no. My mom was afraid that if I went to Wayne State, I would be murdered, because back then Detroit was called the murder capital of the nation, and she wasn’t going to have it. So I went to one of those medical assisting schools, a 6-month program. For me, that was like nothing.

So I came back to my dad and negotiated with him. I said, “If I can’t go to medical school, talk to Mama about letting me go to nursing school.”

Jafar: Did you not have that conversation with her?

Najah: No. Because for my mom — what we say in Lebanese dialect, sit at the bent show as a — my mom would say, “You have to get married, that’s just the way it is.” But that’s not what I was going to do.

Jafar: Why? If that’s the norm all around you, what made you feel like you had to be different than that?

Najah: Well, I wanted to fall in love, first of all. And we were quite sought after in our family, the girls in our family, because we were raised in a very special way. We had all of the etiquette of being hodd. The door was always being knocked on. People came from different countries, but it wasn’t going to happen for me. I just kept saying no. We’d have to do all these kind of crazy things like go make the coffee — like you see in the Turkish films — make the coffee and hand them the coffee. I was like, “What is this? I’m not on display here.” That’s what I’d be thinking in my head. But I understood my mom’s heart and my grandmother’s heart. I understood that that’s what they wanted.

But I wanted to go to college, so my dad negotiated the deal. Luckily, we had moved to Dearborn Heights, and Henry Ford Community College was literally a 5-minute drive from the house. They had at the time one of the best clinical nursing programs in the country, and it was only 2 years. So my dad convinced my mom, and that journey began.

Jafar: Does that mess up your relationship with your mom at all? You negotiate with Dad knowing that Mom’s against the idea. But your mom doesn’t take it in a bad way, because you negotiated with your dad and your dad’s the one that talked to her?

Najah: No, not at all. And once I started, she was the biggest fan. The biggest proponent. I still had to do everything, all of my responsibilities — because I cooked, I cleaned, I was married to the kitchen sink. I’m one of those girls who was doused in how to be what we call sit bit. If you were to come into the house, it’s pretty clean, pretty spotless. Everything’s always in the right tub. There are ways that my mom taught me to be. Syrian women are very much like that. They have a lot of different adabs about the household. We were taught all of that. Even my brothers were taught that, not just the girls. So that was still there, and I’d pull all-nighters and study. I started to take care of Sam. I didn’t neglect any of my family responsibilities toward my parents. My mom at that time was working with my dad in a business that they had.

I remember putting up 206 bones of our human body and having to study them. I remember my mom coming in — my mom was illiterate, but my mom is brilliant, by the way, masha Allah. You would never know that she was not literate. She is brilliant. She would just look at those bones taped up on the wall. I honestly think, had she had the opportunity to go to school — we always said, “Mom, if you had the opportunity to go to school, you’d be in the White House.” That’s how brilliant she is. I think what she saw was the opportunity that she could not have. And then she was fully, fully supportive.

Jafar: They tend to be like that. When they don’t go to school, they tend to be very brilliant at everything else. They tend to be very attentive, a lot of attention to detail.

Najah: Yeah, masha Allah. Jafar, I swear to goodness, if you ever wanted to do the best interview of your show, it’s her.

Jafar: Would she be willing to do it?

Najah: 94 years old, she probably would. She always says, “Najah, put me on TV.”

Jafar: I would love to.

Najah: She has so much to say about this country, about growing up here, about US policy, about presidents, about voting, about the role of women, about how to raise your children. She is by far — Mom is a package deal, masha Allah. I know I’m bragging a lot about my mom, because she deserves it. She’s incredible.

Jafar: There are specific people that built the diaspora community from within their homes, and they produced very remarkable people. Those are the stories that really need to be shared. Like your mom. I don’t blame you for talking about your mom in such a way, because in a way you really understand and can grasp the ripple effect that she had on the entire community.

Najah: She really did. And not only that, I’m watching her habit on the entire family. Just yesterday, we’re all together for dinner, many of my siblings — not all of us, but some. Everybody just dropped what they were doing. We all came and sat in the family room like little kids, and Mom was just monologuing. We’re all just listening to her. She’s very captivating that way. By the way, I’m not the only person who feels this way about her. Anyone who meets her feels the same way about her. She’s very captivating, masha Allah.

But she was very tough. Very strict. And you better have lined up. But if you did something wrong, she never defended you. And she would always say that. Now she says what’s wrong with the way people raise their children is the mothers and the fathers defend their kids. They go to school and they tell the teacher, “The teacher’s wrong. My child is right.” That would never have happened in my mom’s day. What’s wrong is wrong and what’s right is right, and it doesn’t matter who that person is. She drew a hard line with that.

Jafar: Then maybe we can get the setup. That’d be great. You might have to come to her house to do the interview.

Najah: That’s fine.

Jafar: That’s completely fine. We’ll take the setup there.

The Iraqi Refugees and the Baby on the Ventilator

Jafar: There’s this moment in time when you were working, and you really got a bad taste in your mouth for poverty, and what poverty does to people. Can you talk to me about that story?

Najah: Yeah, it’s a well-known story, well documented in Zaman. But the thing about it is, we grew up in poverty. In the south end of Dearborn, we were the poor kids, but we never knew we were poor.

Jafar: That’s a very common theme from the guests that were raised up in the south end. They all say we were broke, no money, but we would never know that we didn’t have money.

Najah: Which is phenomenal. And I say that’s because we were so loved. None of us knew that we were the poor kids in Dearborn. We never knew that.

There are so many stories in medicine that happened that were just life-pivoting, life-changing, and literally life-changing. But the one story that you’re talking about — I took this job at a hospital here in Dearborn, not because I wanted to. Subhanallah, it’s because I didn’t work. I wasn’t a working mom. I was a stay-at-home mom. My husband was working. He was traveling across the world. That’s where his work was, mostly overseas. So I was home with the kids. I wanted to raise my own children. I started a glove company at the house — emptied 53-foot semis, took the kids in a minivan, and I’d go sell medical gloves and masks, things like that, to make a little bit of a living to help out our family. But I stayed home.

So when I decided to go back to work, Yusuf was already four. I’m not a big proponent of putting kids in school early — I’ll do a whole other show with you just on parenting, because I have very strong feelings about how we’re raising our children these days. But to make a long story short, I negotiated a deal with the hospital. I said, “If you allow me to build the first national program for Muslim health care, then I will give you 24-hour, 24/7 coverage. I’ll be on call for Muslim patients and some of the issues that they’re having, because the hospital was struggling with that. But I’m my own boss. I come and I go when I need to. I want to drop off my children at school, and I need to pick them up.” They gave me that flexibility, and that’s how we landed the job. It’s not because I actually wanted to go work there. It was some of the goals that I had for my personal life and my professional life.

By this time, I had gotten a bachelor’s degree in nursing. I went to school when the kids were going to school. It was fun. We packed their backpacks. My A papers were on the refrigerator, theirs too. We’d study together. It was just such a blast. With four kids. So you can do these things.

This story in particular: I became what’s called a clinical transcultural nurse. At that time there were only 33 in the world. None were clinical. They were just transcultural nurses. Those are nurses who graduated with a bachelor’s or more and taken an area of study where you learn a model of approach. It’s a transcultural model that helps you cross cultural barriers and deliver health care. It was designed so that nurses could go anywhere in the world and work in any village to deliver health care. Well, I said to myself, the village is here in Detroit. Why do I have to go around the world? Everyone lives here, and people come from all around the world. So I decided to design the model here.

That was when the Iraqi refugees were coming to the United States in big numbers, and Dearborn was receiving a lot of them. They had a lot of struggles when they first arrived here. We’re not seeing these struggles now, because they’re having first- and second-generation children. But the first ones to arrive, who came from the Gulf War, who lost everything and lived under such difficult situations and in refugee camps — they arrived and many of them were very ill. Many of them had cancers that were not describable. They had anomalies. Their babies were being born with strange anomalies. It was a difficult time for them. There was a lot of loss.

And that is the story. I was taking care of an Iraqi family. By the way, I was no longer a bedside nurse. I wasn’t doing shifts. What I was doing is solving ethical issues, medical ethical issues. I got a subspecialty in medical ethics. So I was solving problems for patients and writing policy for Muslim health care.

This particular couple had twins. The first one died, the second one remained alive. By the time that second infant was a couple of months old, it had never left the neonatal intensive care unit. There was a lot of judgment about the parents, which broke my heart. Like, “She doesn’t come to see her children.” Well, of course she doesn’t come to see her children. They have one car, her husband’s working, he brings her when he can. But there are a lot of opinions about people who are different. So I was very supportive of that family, because they’d lost their first one, and the second one wasn’t going to make it. The second one was blind at birth. The guts were born outside the belly. On a ventilator. And subhanallah al-adhim — in America, we talk about quality of life versus quantity of life. This baby had a quality of life, because this baby gave birth to the man. Perhaps that was the baby’s purpose for its life. I’m not sure.

But ultimately, the physician wanted to remove the baby from the ventilator. This family, their taqlid was of Sayyid Sistani, and they could not remove that ventilator. So the hospital took it to the ethics team. The ethics team cut me out of the ethics team — I was on the ethics team. They barred me from that particular case. They felt I was too close and not objective. The hospital had every right to remove the ventilator, even by state law. I couldn’t see it. So that justice in me — I said, “We’ll figure it out. We can’t do this.” A, it’s bad for the family that they’d have to live with that. And B, it’s a PR nightmare for the hospital too, because the Iraqi families were getting sick and tired of the way they were treated there. And I don’t blame them.

So long story short, I managed — I think by the grace of God, honestly, because this is not a typical way for a story to unravel. But the neonatal intensivist, she said, “I can’t treat this baby anymore in good conscience. I can’t. Because this baby is just suffering.” So she’s the one who called for the ethics case and the removal of the ventilator. I got involved heavily in the case, and I managed to use the nursing model that I was trained in. It teaches you, Jafar, three key principles, and I use them always in my life. How do you accommodate something? How do you negotiate something? And how do you repattern something? So I used the power of the theory I was trained in, and I got the baby home — off the ventilator, with a little bit of hospice support. And hospice does not support vented babies.

Jafar: How were you able to do that?

Najah: I called a friend of mine who was a director of hospice. I explained the situation. I said, “The baby’s not going to make it long. I need a couple days of hospice support, because that’s the only way I could get the baby home.” And she did it. God inspired her. I don’t know, because it’s not supposed to happen.

Najah: I went to visit the family, and that’s how the story begins. The house was empty. I thought they were moving in. They weren’t moving in. That’s all they had. The dignity in their eyes till this moment makes me cry. It was so powerful. Just complete dignity.

Jafar: What do you mean by dignity?

Najah: You could see it in their eyes. They were so grateful. I’m looking around again, thinking that they had moved in — there was nothing in the house. But I had to see the baby formula, because I was there on behalf of the hospital. They had the baby formula in a white Styrofoam cooler with some ice on it. That’s how the formula was kept. It’s in a very powerful video on the Zaman website right now, because we finally told the Zaman story.

There were these white towels, all folded nicely, but I remember the tags on them. So I remember thinking, “Those are new towels,” for a split second. And the baby was lying on top of the towels on the ventilator with the tube feeding. The parents were just so calm and so full of grace and so patient.

I was in shock. I honestly thought to myself, “Did we do the right thing?” My brain flipped to “What are they doing?” And then it flipped back. I realized — I don’t know how long it took me to realize, this all happened so fast — that they built a crib for the baby. And nobody else had anything.

Jafar: That was their crib?

Najah: That was their crib. So I’m in my white nursing coat. I’ve got my stethoscope around my neck. There are these positions of power that we take, or influence, even in the way we dress. I made sure everything was okay. I promised them I would bury the baby. And I left. I got to the front porch. The house is here in Dearborn, and sometimes when I’m here, I just go drive past it. And I just cried. I made my way to my minivan and I called my mom. I’m like, “Mom, you will not believe what I just saw.” My mom was moving, so I told her. My mom has always been a humanitarian — probably where I get it from. She told me, “Come. Take whatever you want. We’re moving. Your father’s buying me new things anyway.” Which is true.

So I went. I rented a U-Haul, and myself and my four kids, we went and we just picked everything up from my mom. I completely outfitted that house within 24 hours. Furniture, beds, mattresses, silverware, pots, pans, everything you can imagine a house needed. Maybe a little more than 24 hours, but within a day or so. And then the baby died, like that third or fourth day. So then we proceeded to bury the baby.

The Mother Whose Baby Came to Her in Dreams

Jafar: Why was it an important promise for you to tell them, “I promise you I’ll get the baby buried”? What happened?

Najah: This isn’t something I’ve spoken about publicly very much. What was happening to the Iraqi refugees broke my heart in pieces. One day I was at the Joy Road Mosque. It was Ashura. A woman came to me and she said, “Are you Najah Bazzy?” I said, “Yes.” I had a youth group, people knew who I was. She said to me, “Did you bury my baby?” I said to her in Arabic, “I don’t understand.” She said, “The hospital told me you buried my baby. And she’s coming to me in my dreams. And she’s crying. And I want to go see her.”

I said, “Give me your name. I did not bury your baby, but let me find out who did.” I took her name, went to the hospital, got to the record, and found out that her baby had been cremated.

I was out of my mind. You can imagine. Out of my mind. I was furious. I told the hospital — I found out how it happened — I told the hospital, “I refuse, and I will not defend what happened here. You need to tell this lady what happened.”

Jafar: They told the lady, after her baby died, that you buried her, while they went and cremated the baby?

Najah: No, not exactly. What happened was, there’s a form. It’s called the green disposition form in a hospital. You have to sign it when there’s a fetal demise, a miscarriage, or whatever. The parents have to sign it. Well, a lot of the Iraqi refugees did not know what it was. How are they supposed to know, because nobody translated it for them?

So what I came to learn was, not just her baby — many babies had been cremated, because Medicaid only paid $75 toward an infant burial. You could only cremate for $75. So whoever was giving these moms who lost their children this green disposition paper, they were not explaining it correctly. These babies were going to a special lab that was at the University of Michigan. That particular lab was a research lab on infant demises. It was a pathology lab. Then those babies become a ward of the state. Well, every 6 months these babies — whomever they were collected from, wherever they were — would then be collectively, ceremonially cremated.

So when I found out this was happening, I of course called for a major-league meeting at the highest level of authority, got to the bottom of it, had the forms translated, started a translation department with medically trained translators — so that people who spoke any language, not just Muslims, but who did not speak English, would know what they were signing. And changed policy. I one time chased a hearse on the way to Ann Arbor and recaptured a baby, because the same thing happened. So that led to infant burial for me. I started a program. I was burying babies, because I didn’t want these babies to get cremated. It was devastating.

Jafar: The baby coming to her in her dreams is a very unsettling thing.

Najah: Oh my goodness. If her name was Fatima, or the baby’s name was Fatima. But these moments — like the baby on the ventilator, and this situation with this mom — I can’t turn away from these things. I cannot. I will not. Who would I be if I were to turn away from these things? I couldn’t do it. You have to fix things.

Jafar: Did you ever end up going to that lab and seeing those babies that they did research on?

Najah: No. But it gets worse.

200 Babies in Jars and Tubs in the Hospital Basement

Najah: While I started burying these babies, I would just ask the doctors, “Doctor so-and-so, I need $500. Can you help me bury this baby?” Of course, no one said no.

That is the responsibility of a mother. My mother would take care of me the same way I would take care of my daughter. There’s no ‘18 and you’re cut off.’ That’s not who we are.” So they had a hard time understanding this interdependence of our family relationships that make us who we are.

Najah: Just teaching about why we do the things we do. Jafar: In one jar?

Najah: Like in a tub. Jars and tubs. And I realized these were all babies. A couple hundred. Like 200. I don’t know the exact number, but something like that. So, of course, another moment in time. What do you do? Again, I assemble the highest authority of the hospital. I meet with them. I’m like, “Do you know what’s downstairs?” They had no idea. I said, “There are all these fetuses stored in these jars and these tubs. And they’re all in formaldehyde.” They were from 1995. This is 2002 — 1995, 1996, 1997. That green disposition paper I told you about had not been signed. Without it being signed, you can’t do anything with those body parts. Even at Wayne County morgue right now, there are people there who have still been there for a long time, because no one has claimed them. So you can’t do anything with it.

Jafar: But what does the formaldehyde do?

Najah: It preserves. It’s a preservative. So, again, a moment in time. I asked them what we should do, because that’s a PR nightmare for the hospital, and God knows who these babies are. So, long story short, I went through the list. There was one baby whose name was Ahmed. So I asked if I could bury Ahmed. They said yes. I buried Ahmed. All the rest got cremated. I could not live with myself. It was so hard for me. It’s just so crazy. I’m like, “How could this be happening?”

So I started Plots for Tots, which is a second program. We started the program with the baby — we called that baby Zahra, and that’s another story, that I named that from an Iraqi man that was dying in the hospital. I’ll tell you that story in a minute. This is not going to happen on my watch.”

Washing the Dead Since 15, and a Hadith That Is a Call to Action

Jafar: How do you process your emotions in that moment? As you’re saying the story right now, I got goosebumps. I felt my stomach turn. And you’re telling me a story that happened over 20 years ago. In the moment, being with Teresa, holding the fetus in your hand — at first, what did you think she wanted you for? Because she wasn’t acting weird, she wasn’t talking.

Najah: I had no idea what she wanted me for, none whatsoever. But I’ve been washing the dead since I was 15. So the death and dying process is a very natural part of my life. It’s not anything unusual for me.

Jafar: At 15 you started washing the dead bodies? How did you get into that?

Najah: The aunt that I told you about — the aunt that was kissing the baby — her father taught her how to do ghusl al-mayyit. There weren’t many people in the community that could do that. We Are Created to Serve

Talents That Never Leave, Just Get Rerouted

Najah: So if you can identify it early — I did it for myself. I asked Mama. I said, “Mama, even though we were poor, we didn’t have anything. The Goodfellows used to knock on the door to give us Christmas gifts to all the kids in the South End.” I asked her, “Mama, what did I used to play with?” Because I remember, and I just wanted to validate. I remember giving my brother the odd shots all the time. He was younger than me by 3 years. He was the patient. Never wanted anything else.

My father’s army kit was my toy. He was a medic in the army, and he had the surgical kit. It was green canvas. I can still smell it. There’s first, second, third base. There’s a bench. Not everyone can step up to the plate first all together. Not everyone can do that. But what has to happen in a healthy family is each person gets to get up and bat and run the bases while others sit on the bench and support, including the mom. The husband has to sit on the bench, and he has to let his wife get up to the plate and hit a home run and run her bases. And she has to sit on the bench and let him do it. And each kid has a chance to get up to bat while both parents are sitting on the bench. And each sibling has to sit at the bench. That’s how you build a family. That’s how it goes. But for everyone to be doing it all at the same time is impossible. It’s impossible.

And these women are killing themselves, and men are killing themselves, and everyone’s so stressed out, because everyone’s trying to get up to the plate at the same time. Someone has to swing, someone has to catch. Someone has to sit on the bench. That’s a healthy family. And we have to learn to be patient with it.

I didn’t get my bachelor’s degree till I was 36.

Jafar: Really?

Najah: Yes, because I was at home. Just taking care of the kids. Didn’t have the time or the money. But it’s okay. I got it. You’re going to get there with it or without it. So you may as well just take your time and get there with it. I learned that from a biology teacher. She’s like, “You’re going to get it. You can be a PhD if you want to be.” She’s telling the whole class, “And you should be. There’s going to come a time where you’re going to be that person with it or without it. So it’s your choice to be with it, even if it takes you forever to get it. It’s your choice.”

So when it was my time to bat, the kids supported me, and I got up to bat. Ali’s traveled the world. When it was his time to bat, I supported that. So it’s just about balance. It’s about balance and self-sacrifice. And it’s about those difficult conversations to have when you’re looking for a partner. If you find a supportive partner that is willing to sit on the bench and watch you bat, and vice versa — it’s a team. You just talked about teams. Family is a team. That’s what it is.

I grew up with parents who were a team. We were a team to take care of my brother Sammy. Everybody had to pitch in. All the grandchildren had to pitch in. Sammy’s life for us gave us that sense of team. When we’re in the hospital and someone’s sick, we’re all there. We’re all together. My husband’s family is the same way. They’re a team. They stand up for each other. We’ve just learned that. It’s just beautiful. And it’s not hard, but it requires self-sacrifice.

Sammy’s Legacy Without Moving

Jafar: Having spent so much time with Sammy, was it more difficult grasping the idea of him passing away?

Najah: Yes and no. He lived way beyond his years. He lived to be 70 almost. The doctors told Mama he would die in year one. He did not. Year seven, he did not. 13 was the max. He did not. So we watched this man — we all got married, we all had children. He tutored all of our kids, even though he couldn’t move. By the time my dad got ill, Sammy was the man of the house, even though he couldn’t move. He used his eyeballs to move the mouse, because he couldn’t use anything, and ordered everything online. He was my best partner to take care of all of my parents’ pharmacy needs. Everything he did online. He golfed online. He was a major golfer online. He was president of the Muscular Dystrophy Association adult camp. Set it all up, everything, with the movement of his eyes.

If he can do that and not move, what am I supposed to do if I can move?

Jafar: So you were kind of almost happy for him.

Najah: Well, happy in the sense that I do believe Sammy’s body is off — it’s beautiful that he’s walking with no wheelchair. That he’s with the family having a blast. I really do believe that. So yeah, I’m very happy for him. But what he left behind, his legacy, is incredible. Incredible. Sammy was a teacher without moving. His funeral was 1,000 people. All walks of life.

Jafar: Wow.

Najah: Yeah, he was huge. And he couldn’t move. What do you say about that? Imagine what we’re supposed to do with a functioning body.

Jafar: Exactly. What are we supposed to do?

Najah: I always say, because he could not move, I did move.

Jafar: Yeah.

Najah: It’s hard to hold me still. But yeah, so I think that there’s a way to balance family life. And I’ve noticed a lot of women who were full-time — they bought into the whole economic dream of making a lot of money. A lot of them are backing down and working part-time now, because they get it. They’re like, “We’re being sold a bunch of unreal expectations on women.” I feel for them, and I applaud them. Because they’re taking back ownership of their motherhood. They’re taking back ownership of what it means to be a spouse. They’re taking back ownership of their career choices. Good for them. Good for them.

Because you know what? This world can applaud you, but then they’re not there when you’re going to be in need. It’s going to be your children. Not a person in corporate America is probably going to read you the Fatiha or come to your grave. It’s your children.

Jafar: Right.

Najah: So who are we trying to impress? Why? Just tell me why.

Knowing When It’s Time to Leave

Najah: One of the last days I had at work, I remember that I went to my supervisor — and I’m an executive by now. She said to me, “When I do your evaluation, it’s really difficult.” I said, “Why?” She’s like, “Well, I can’t give you 100% on everything.” And I looked at her across the desk, and she’s like, “I have to find something that you can improve in.” I thought to myself, and I laughed. I’m like, “Geez, this is so wrong. That’s not how I would want to treat anyone.” And so I left.

This is another thing. When you start driving around the parking lot of your employment and you don’t want to go in, it’s time to go. Money is good, because it buys you things. But my brother, my eldest brother, Allah yerhamu — he just passed away — he used to say to me, “Money in the hands of good people can be great. And money in the hands of bad people can be awful. But in the end, it’s just a tool.” And that’s true. But can money buy you true happiness? No. It buys you things. Do those things make you happy? Yes, temporarily. But happiness comes from inside out. It comes from deep within. It comes from you loving yourself, accepting yourself. It comes from forgiving other people. It comes from love of God. And these are all works in progress. I’m not just getting here. You evolve into these things.

Jafar: Right. And it’s a long-time evolution to get there. Every day is a new day, and every day is a new evolution. We’re always a work in progress.

A Death Doula, and the Sting of Grief

Jafar: I don’t know if you know Haider Qazaan, but we had an episode —

Najah: Very much so. I know him very well.

Jafar: We did an episode about washing dead Muslims, and he has been doing it also since he was a teenager. One thing he told me is that his relationship with death has drastically changed. He doesn’t see death as — he sees it as peace, as rahmat for the person, and thus making it very easy when someone passes away, because he doesn’t really see them as gone. They’re just in a different place. I wonder, when you lose your brothers, when you lose your father, do you also look at it that way, or is there still a sting?

Najah: Big sting. Because I believe the more you love, the more it stings. I know where they are. There’s no doubt in my mind about that. We all have an exit ramp. Everyone. We don’t think about the exit ramp. But we all have an exit ramp. When I used to teach end-of-life work — I still teach end-of-life work in the hospitals — they would talk about terminal illness, and I would say, “Let’s stop for a moment. We’re all terminal. Everyone is terminal. Let’s get that straight first. There’s an exit ramp for each one of us.” So we all know we have to leave. But the way God created the system — even though you know that person is with their Lord, and that person inshallah is in a good place, because maybe they suffered tremendously, a tremendous amount of illness, and they’re ready to go — but their whole carbon footprint, their place in your family, that’s still real.

Jafar: Are there conversations that you wish you had, now looking back?

Najah: No.

Jafar: You feel like everything that you should have said, you have told them?

Najah: I always do. Yeah. I’m good at that, I have to say. Making sure there are no words that go unsaid. But grief is grief. And it’s been a struggle, especially with the last one.

Jafar: Which happened last month?

Najah: Yeah, it happened 2 months ago. He said, “I need my sister.” I went to the hospital. He said, “It’s time.” I said, “Okay.”

Jafar: He told you that?

Najah: He said, “I’m not going to be here tomorrow.” I said, “Okay. Let’s get you ready, habibi. Let’s get you ready, albi. Whatever you need.” He said, “I need this, I need this, I need that.” My siblings, his children — we were all together the whole time. We did not leave his side, his grandchildren, no one left. But I stayed next to him.

I don’t know if you know what a doula is. We talk about doulas, these are people who usually birth — like midwives. You can learn to be a doula, which is an assistant during birth. I would say that I’m a death doula. I’ve helped so many people leave this earth in our community. That’s just what I do. And it’s a very calming thing for me. It’s not hard for me. It’s like the responsibility that’s before me. So I talked him through the whole thing. And then one time he woke up at like 3:00 in the morning, or maybe 2:00 in the morning, and he’s like, “Naj” — that’s my family nickname — “What’s taking so long?” I’m like, “You’re ready?” He said, “I’m ready.” I said, “But Allah is not ready for you, habibi. You have to be patient.”

Jafar: Was that the last night?

Najah: Yeah, that was a couple hours before he passed. I read him Quran, loved him. We were stroking his head, having every person come in and say goodbye. I’m able to do all of that, accommodate all of that, until he’s ready to go.

Jafar: How did he know?

Najah: He just knew. But when it comes to death and dying — I always say, even flowers, I can’t keep a flower alive. They all like to die with me. So there’s just this thing that Allah has given me, and that’s the ability to be near people in their dying moments. And it’s a privilege.

Final Conversations and the Serenity of Dying

Jafar: What type of conversations do people like to have in those final moments?

Najah: This has been over 40 years of this work. It does not matter if they’re Muslim, if they’re non-Muslim, what their ethnicity is, what their skin color is. None of that matters, what their gender is. People usually are saying the same thing. And they’re having similar experiences. I don’t want to say too much, because I don’t want to disclose the privacy of some of those moments. They’re not all the same, but they have similarities.

There’s a sense of serenity that comes to the soul. I think that there’s a knowledge that opens before the soul. You always hear people talking about seeing their loved ones. Doesn’t matter who they are. They’ll say, “I can see my father, I can see my so-and-so, I can see my son.” You hear that a lot. People talk about the light. When they’re verbal, people talk about — I remember just a couple of months ago, we lost a woman in our community, and I was at her bedside. She was at home. She was still cognizant. I said to her, “Are you afraid?” She said, “No.” I said, “Have you said everything you need to say?” Her husband was next to me. She said, “Yes.” So I said, “Is there anything that I can do for you?” She said, “No.” I said, “I’m going to stand right here by you, and I’m here for you. Whatever you need.”

That’s just kind of how it goes. People come to the sense of serenity. It’s the only way I can describe it. Almost like — we say you make your peace. People who have forgiveness issues, or people who’ve traumatized them in their lives, a lot of times they need to make those amends. Sometimes they’ll ask for that. Like, “Tell so-and-so this.” Or “Tell so-and-so that.” Or sometimes they tell you secrets. And you hold those things. Those things go to your grave with you. But for the moment, I can tell you that it’s a beautiful transition. No doubt. Beyond a doubt.

Jafar: I’m thinking about final conversations with people that you love the most. And what words could come to mind, but I can’t think of any.

Najah: One of the leadership exercises that I do is, you have to write your obituary. Because you have to begin with your end in mind. And I’ll ask the question in the leadership training: “If you were not here, what would you want someone to say about you? And who would say these words?” And you have to write it down. That’s a very hard exercise for people to do.

Jafar: How come?

Najah: Because you’re facing your mortality. And it’s very fragile. I didn’t do it this year, but normally I teach a Muslim chaplaincy class out of the University of Chicago. These Muslim chaplains, I put them through this leadership training. This is one of the exercises that we do. But I also do it with the youth. I did it at the MYC retreat, but I was easy on them, because they’re young people. I don’t want to scare them. So I’ll say, “If you were not in the room, what would you want someone to say about you?”

People coming in touch with their mortality is a very difficult thing for people. Because our brains, when Allah made them, he made them for survival. In the end, we’re mammals, and we have mammalian brains. And survival is what our brains are trained to do. We have two parts of our brain — our lower brain stem, which is the survival mechanism, and the higher brain, which is the intellect. So it’s natural for that survival instinct to kick in. It’s natural. It’s part of the way God made it. And it’s phenomenal to me, and awesome, like wow, when I see that despite that lower brain fighting for survival, the higher brain kicks in with the intellect — the intellect of the soul, of the heart.

Jafar: And it tells the body to settle, to back down those —

Najah: Somehow it allows it to settle and get this sense of sukoon and sakina and peace. It’s really amazing. So, to your question, I know where they are, and I’m very happy for them, and that’s not the hard part. The hard part is not letting them go. The hard part becomes when you have that hole. But alhamdulillah, this is love, ultimately.

Planning Her Own Exit, Down to 12 Pages

Jafar: Your brother called and said he wanted his younger sister. Who would you say you want?

Najah: I don’t know yet. I haven’t thought about that. I don’t think I would need anybody, because my kids know what I need. They’re raised to know. I wouldn’t need to call anyone, because I think my husband and my kids know. They just would never leave. They know. Whereas I am often called by people who need that. But my inner circle knows.

And I’ve written it all out.

Jafar: Oh, you do?

Najah: Yeah, because I figured, you know what? Why not? I plan events like crazy. I’m an event planner. I mean, I’m really not, but I am. I’m always planning Zaman events, family events. I was like, yeah, I should just plan my own event. Let them know. Why do I have to let my kids guess? Let me just tell them what’s expected, what’s needed. It’s part of motherhood. 12 pages. It’s in a Google Doc now, on my son’s Google Drive. Step by step, what to do. Because I can’t handle disorganization. I can’t handle chaos.

Jafar: Even in your death?

Najah: I can’t handle it. I don’t want people running to carry my casket who don’t really know me. One of the things, when you become more public, is people get extremely attached to you as a public figure. But the bigger the public becomes, the smaller your core becomes. And I’ve just always been a family person. If I go out with a friend, it’s a rare thing, and usually has a deeper reason. I’m just not that person. So for me, I need to have organization. Like, okay, the Zaman folks can carry the casket this time, and the MYC folks can carry it this time, and my cousins and my family carried it — I wanted that, because I’m also aware that people feel a tremendous sense of attachment. I feel privileged to be in a position where people feel that attachment, and I don’t want their grief to be greater than it needs to be. So they need to participate. That’s part of grief. So I want to make sure that everybody has their role to play. I think that’s responsible.

Point of Entry to Point of Exit, and Mentoring the Next Generation

Jafar: And when it’s all said and done, and when that day does come, how do you want people to look back? If you were to answer that question — if you’re not in the room.

Najah: It’s interesting you say that, because I’ve taught them point of entry — you will see kids in our community repeat this all the time, it’s well known. When you think about leadership, you think about life. When you think about life, you think about the point of entrance to the point of exit with everything you’re doing. It’s my leadership model, and I’m developing it into this book. If you’re going to have an event — let’s just say it’s Ashura, let’s say it’s Ramadan — you have to think about the point of entrance. And I’ll challenge them. I’ll say, “What is the point of entrance? You saw that.” Well, “When they come in the hall.” No, that’s not the point of entrance. The point of entrance is when you have an idea, and you want to execute that idea, and that idea starts to manifest, and it starts to grow, and it becomes a concept, and now you’re putting it out in the world. That’s the point of entrance. When it moves from here to —

Jafar: Action. The parking lot, right?

Najah: Yeah. The parking lot, and how people are parking, and then how they’re entering, and who’s greeting them, and who’s seating them, and how do they feel, and do they feel welcomed, and who’s the speaker, and does the speaker have water — all these little details about hospitality and about care of others. That is how I’ve trained these young people in our community to think. And then, when they leave, what’s the point of exit? What are they leaving with? What knowledge have they gained? How do they feel? Who did they meet? Were they injured — emotionally? Because sometimes speakers can injure people too, unintentionally, but something can hurt. Are we checking on them? What’s happening?

So they’re trained, so when I’m not here, I think they will be laughing about point of entrance to point of exit, and master plans, because that’s what I’ve drilled into everybody that knows me. “Show me your master plan.”

Jafar: So when you’re not in the room, you want people to think of their master plan.

Najah: Yeah, I want them to think about the master plan. I hope they also think about mentorship. The mentor that I had — I was 15, she was the youth leader. The reason why I started the whole youth movement was because I wanted my kids to have what I had. There was a vacuum in our community when we didn’t have a person like that. So I want them to all give back, and I see it happening already. These kids that were raised with me from ‘97, they’re all like in their 40s now, and they all have children. You know so many of them. Between that first group and now, there are over 300 of them — and I’m just talking about the actual leadership group, like 10 to 12 of them every couple of years. Then they rotate out, then there’s a new batch that comes in. It’s fun to watch them grow. I’m very proud of every single one of them. When I listen to their memories, they talk about the retreats, and they talk about this memory and that memory. “Do you remember this, and do you remember that?” That’s what they’re going to carry with them, but that’s what I want them also to give back to the next generation. And I know that they will, because they’re already doing that.
