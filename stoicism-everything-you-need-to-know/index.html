Stoicism Is Not an Ethical Philosophy

The most common modern picture of Stoicism gets it wrong, the scholar John Sellers argues, not because the popular image is false but because it captures only a fragment. Today the word “stoic” conjures a way of enduring hardship without complaint, and Stoicism is filed under self-help. That ethical dimension is real, Sellers says, but ideas like that “don’t just spring up out of nowhere.” Stoicism was a complete philosophical system with a sophisticated logic, a physics and metaphysics covering the nature of the world and the existence of God, and an ethics that was supposed to fall out of those foundations.

To call Stoicism an ethical philosophy, O’Connor suggests, is like answering “what is Christianity?” by saying it’s where you treat people as your brother and don’t have sex before marriage. Christians do those things, but the resurrection and the existence of God are the heart of the matter, and the ethics are one consequence of a much larger story. Sellers agrees completely. He would not call Stoicism an ethical philosophy at all, “because there’s so much more going on.”

Socrates, the Cynics, and Zeno▶ 0:50

If anyone is the founder of Stoic ethics, Sellers says, it is Socrates, who functions as a kind of “grandfather figure.” Socrates was the first Greek philosopher to put forward the idea that a virtuous character, and only a virtuous character, is the key to living a good life. The Stoics took up that famous Socratic claim and built on it.

The figure usually credited with founding the school, though, is Zeno of Citium, not to be confused with the earlier Zeno of Elea of paradox fame. Zeno came from Cyprus to Athens, and there is a famous, probably apocryphal, origin story. Sent on a business trip by his father with a ship full of cargo, he was shipwrecked near the port of Piraeus and lost everything. Dragging himself ashore, he wandered into a bookshop and began reading Xenophon’s memoir of Socrates. Struck by it, he asked the bookseller where he could find men like Socrates. At that moment a Cynic philosopher walked past, and the bookseller said: follow that man.

That detail joins the dots, Sellers explains. The Cynics were a school from the same period whose most famous member, Diogenes, threw social custom to the wind, lived in a barrel, and told Alexander the Great only to get out of his sunlight. Plato had called Diogenes “Socrates gone mad,” and the description is apt: the Cynics took the Socratic emphasis on virtuous character and pushed it to an extreme, developing a complete indifference to everything else. Sold into slavery, Diogenes is said to have shrugged that his virtuous character was still intact, so why should it bother him. With that came a rejection of all social conventions and the ideal of living according to nature rather than according to custom or law, another idea the Stoics inherited.

Zeno studied the Cynic philosophy but decided it was not for him. He then spent a reported ten years at Plato’s Academy, learning Platonism, and also studied with the Megarians, a group of Socratic philosophers with strong interests in logic. Eventually, wanting to be neither a Cynic, a Platonist, nor a Megarian, he began teaching under his own name at the Painted Stoa in the centre of Athens, drawing together elements from everyone he had studied with. The school took its name from that colonnade.

Three Stages, and a Skewed Inheritance▶ 8:18

Stoicism developed in stages. It begins with Zeno in Athens around 300 BC. His successor Cleanthes was especially interested in physics, and after him came Chrysippus, traditionally regarded as the most important early Stoic, the man who pulled the threads together into a substantial system. He reportedly wrote seventy-five books, almost all now lost, so the early period survives only as fragments and secondhand reports. Famous in antiquity as a logician, Chrysippus was arguably the second great logician of the ancient world after Aristotle. These Athenian Stoics built a rich and broad system spanning metaphysics, physics, epistemology, logic, theories of grammar, and ethics, comparable to anything in the Platonic or Aristotelian traditions.

That living tradition in Athens started to fade around the beginning of the first century BC, when the Roman general Sulla besieged the city and many philosophers scattered to Rhodes, Alexandria, Rome, and elsewhere. A few decades later the Roman statesman Cicero wrote a series of philosophical works that translated Stoic ideas into Latin for the first time. Because so much of the earlier material is lost, Cicero’s accounts are now among the earliest, fullest, and most complete sources for early Stoic thought.

After Cicero came the famous Romans: Seneca, Epictetus, and Marcus Aurelius. The traditional story holds that they cared only about applied ethics, not theory, and that the Athenians were the real theoreticians. Sellers wants to challenge both stereotypes and stress the continuity across the tradition. The early Athenians were not only building complex theory but also seeking, with the existential urgency they inherited from Socrates and the Cynics, to learn how to live well. The later Romans, in turn, were reading those early texts and trying to put their more complex theoretical ideas into practice. What has given us such a skewed view, he argues, is the contingency of textual transmission: the works that happened to survive are the ethical ones a reader can buy in a bookshop today, so people naturally assume that is what Stoicism is.

That contingency matters for another reason. Much of what we know about the lost early Stoics comes not from fragments but from their opponents, who quote them in order to criticise them. It is, O’Connor notes, a bit like reconstructing the Bible from quotations in Richard Dawkins’s The God Delusion: you could piece together the outline, but not a sympathetic picture. The same caution applies throughout, Sellers says, though a broad picture of their worldview can still be recovered.

Logic: Propositions, Not Just Terms▶ 23:09

Sellers’s own academic introduction to Stoicism begins not with ethics but with logic. The Stoics divided philosophy into three areas, logic, physics, and ethics, and taught them in different orders at different times, so it would be a mistake to treat logic as the single foundation. At least one Stoic put physics last, with theology as the grand finale of a philosophical education. The system is better understood as interdependent: all three parts are needed, and the order of learning is incidental.

In formal logic, Chrysippus developed what we now call Stoic syllogistic, and it differed sharply from Aristotle’s. An Aristotelian syllogism deals in terms, words like “cat,” “horse,” or “human,” joined by quantifiers such as “all,” “some,” and “no”: all humans are animals, all animals are mortal, therefore all humans are mortal. Chrysippus instead worked with whole propositions: if it is raining, I will take my umbrella; it is raining; therefore I will take my umbrella. If P then Q, P, therefore Q. Aristotelian logic, built on terms and quantifiers, dominated the medieval period and felt like simply what logic was. The Stoic, propositional approach was largely forgotten, surfacing in the work of the medieval logician Peter Abelard and then revived by Frege. Modern formal logic, with its mathematisation, owes far more to the Stoics than to Aristotle, making Chrysippus strikingly ahead of his time.

When the Stoics spoke of “logic,” however, they meant something far broader than formal argument. Logic covered everything to do with logos, a Greek word meaning reason, argument, and word, and so language in general, and it included epistemology, the theory of where our ideas come from.

Empiricism and the Rejection of Innate Ideas▶ 25:06

The Stoics were empiricists. All knowledge, they held, comes through the senses. The mind receives information from the world, translates it into propositions, and then either assents to a proposition or rejects it. You see a cat on the mat; your mind is presented with the proposition “the cat is on the mat”; you decide whether to assent. If you assent, you form the belief, and if the belief is true, you have knowledge. The formal logic described above is then constructed out of these propositions. Provided the senses are reliable, assent is given correctly, and the arguments are valid, knowledge comes out the other end.

This anticipates later empiricism remarkably closely. John Locke famously described the newborn mind as a blank sheet of paper on which experience writes, but the Stoics had already said that at birth the mind is like a blank sheet on which impressions stamp themselves. A corollary, Sellers notes, is that there are no innate ideas, which directly challenges Plato’s view that we possess innate knowledge and merely recollect it through experience.

Universals: The Stoics as Early Nominalists▶ 28:12

Plato’s challenge of universals presses on any empiricist. Two chairs sit in different places, made of different material, yet both are chairs; they share something. Plato’s answer was the Forms: a perfect realm in which individual things participate, with the physical world a mere imperfect reflection, and knowledge a kind of recollection from a premortal acquaintance with the Forms.

The Stoics rejected all of this, but then faced the same problem. Their answer, like that of modern empiricists, was that the concept “chair” is something we construct after the fact, on the basis of experiencing many similar things. The concept comes after experience, not before it. Plato had argued that you could not even recognise two objects as chairs unless you already possessed the concept; the Stoics replied that we simply develop these concepts as we go. Categories like “table” and “chair” are products of convention, labels we apply to matter arranged in different ways. There is no real category of “table,” only the name we give it, a position the modern world calls nominalism. This makes the Stoics one of the earliest schools of nominalism, though Sellers notes that Diogenes the Cynic had already needled Plato along these lines, saying he could see tables but never the idea of a table, so the seed may be part of the Cynic inheritance.

Aristotle also rejected Plato’s Forms, but differently. He allowed something like what modern philosophers call natural kinds. Artificial categories such as chairs may be arbitrary, but the distinction between, say, birds and horses is a fact of nature, not an arbitrary label. For Aristotle there is a form of horse, visible in individual horses themselves, not existing independently of them but consisting in a real shared set of properties. The Stoics declined to talk about forms in quite that sense, though they did grant that objects have certain qualities; they simply resisted giving forms any existence beyond particulars. Both Aristotle and the Stoics, being empiricists interested in particular things, can therefore look surprisingly modern next to the popular image of Plato.

Materialism, Matter, and Breath▶ 35:18

Turning to metaphysics, the Stoics were materialists: famously, “only bodies exist.” Connecting this to their nominalism, they also held that only particulars exist, no universals and no Platonic Forms. Particular individual physical objects are the entire architecture of the world.

But they complicated the picture. Just as Aristotle analysed objects into matter and form, his hylomorphism, the Stoics held that everything is a combination of two things. One they called matter, the passive element. The other, the active element, they called pneuma, meaning breath or, in later tradition, spirit. The crucial difference from Aristotle is that pneuma is itself material. Both principles are material: a passive matter pervaded by a material active principle that gives it structure or, in living things, life. A human being is a body of matter wholly permeated by breath, which the Stoics identified with the soul that makes us alive.

Crucially, this breath is in everything, including inanimate objects. In a table it is what gives the object its hardness and structure, what holds it together so that it is not just a pile of dust. The breath always carries a degree of tension, and the higher the tension, the more complex the object. The most basic tension gives cohesion to a chair or table; a higher level gives plants their principle of life; higher still gives animals perception and movement; and the highest gives human beings rationality. This is often called the scale of nature. There is no radical difference in kind between a human mind, an animal mind, and what goes on in a plant or a stone, only different levels of complexity, with breath supplying the organisation that makes each thing what it is.

The parallel with Aristotle’s theory of soul is close. Aristotle too held that all living things have souls, with plants possessing the lowest level (nutrition and growth), animals adding locomotion, and humans adding rationality, and for him a soul was not a magical inhabitant but essentially a form of matter. The word psuchē, translated as soul, was common philosophical currency, and the term carries no mystery: the difference between a living human and a corpse is that one has soul and one does not, soul being simply whatever explains why a thing is alive. The Stoics extend the same continuum not just up to humans but all the way down to artefacts like tables and chairs. Where Aristotle would say the corpse has lost its soul, the Stoics would say both the living being and the corpse have breath, but the corpse has lost much of the tension and complexity in how that breath is organised.

Reason All the Way Down▶ 44:15

This active principle is also called logos, reason, so for the Stoics reason permeates everything; there is a rationality even to how the inanimate world operates. O’Connor draws the connection to modern debates in philosophy of mind, where panpsychism and idealism hold that consciousness is present foundationally in the universe in a rudimentary form, becoming full-blown only when complexly arranged. Sellers sees a genuine parallel.

The Stoics, in fact, used this against their rivals. Their great competitors in Athens were the Epicureans, reductive atomists for whom the world is dead, inert matter randomly colliding, leaving consciousness as an emergent property somehow arising from lifeless atoms. The Stoics already raised the standard objection: how could piling up enough dead matter suddenly produce consciousness? Their own argument ran the other way. If human beings are rational and animate, then rationality and animation must already belong to nature as a whole; what we have cannot be a purely emergent property but must already be present in nature. So they explicitly rejected reductive materialism.

This points to a difference between two ways of being a materialist. A modern reductionist drags the high-flying things, consciousness, justice, universals, down to the level of atoms and physics. The Stoics do something closer to the reverse, elevating the natural world into a higher plane of reason and logos. For this reason, while “materialist” is not wrong, Sellers suggests “naturalist” may be the better word: it is only things within the natural world that exist, but in both the Stoic and Aristotelian cases something more than inert matter is needed to understand what is going on. O’Connor adds that the modern preference is often for “physicalism,” the reductive view, with “naturalism” leaving more room for the kind of elevated nature the Stoics had in mind.

God Identified with Nature▶ 48:41

The talk of breath pervading the universe sounds religious, and it is. The Stoics did believe in God, and exactly as Spinoza would much later, they identified God with nature, or more precisely with the active principle that animates nature. God is Zeus is logos is divine breath is divine fire; they have endless names for the same thing. God is the soul of the world, the active animate breath that animates all of nature, just as the breath within us is our soul. One consequence is that our individual souls are fragments of this divine soul that animates the whole. The Stoics are pantheists, with God immanent in the world, and because all of this stays within the natural world, it remains consistent with the claim that only physical bodies exist.

The Cosmos: Conflagration, Eternal Recurrence, and the Void▶ 53:25

Here the system grows stranger, into the cosmology that modern fans of Stoicism tend to ignore but which is genuinely part of the theory. If nature is God and a living being, then nature is alive and, like any living being, has a life cycle. The Stoic view is that the world is born at a certain moment, runs its cycle, and is then destroyed. Both birth and destruction are characterised as the passive matter being entirely consumed by the active principle, identified with reason, God, breath, and, in an idea taken from Heraclitus, fire. The whole world is consumed in a kind of singularity, everything becoming pure active principle, pure God, and then reborn like a phoenix into another cycle.

Each cycle, the Stoics held, is identical. They never used the phrase, but they effectively held the doctrine of eternal recurrence: if a perfectly rational divine principle organises the world, what it produces will be perfect, so there is no reason to change it, and you get exactly the same cycle every time. This raises the logical puzzles the Stoics loved, such as whether the Socrates of one cycle is the same as the Socrates of the next, and whether identical cycles can even be distinguished. For materialists and empiricists, these are remarkably theological and fanciful views about cosmology.

They were also specific about the shape and size of the world. The single unified nature identified with God is spherical, probably with the earth at its centre, as Aristotle held. When it is consumed in the conflagration it expands and so needs room to grow, which means there must be void outside nature for it to expand into and contract from. Aristotle, by contrast, thought the spherical world had nothing beyond its limits. The Stoics drew on a famous objection, raised by the ancient philosopher Archytas, against that idea: imagine a person at the edge of the universe stretching out an arm. Either the arm passes through, in which case there is something beyond, or it is stopped, in which case there must be something beyond to stop it. Either way there must be something outside, an argument O’Connor likens to the moment in The Truman Show when the protagonist reaches the edge of his world.

Incorporeals: Real but Not Existent▶ 59:46

The void creates a tension. If only bodies exist, what about the void, which is precisely empty of matter? The Stoics recognised four such items they called incorporeals: time, place, void, and the lekton, the meaning of propositions. A defining mark of a body is that it can act and be acted upon, causally interacting with other things, which is why the soul must be physical, since it has causal power. The void makes nothing happen, so it can be incorporeal. Likewise, the physical story of sound, vibrations in the air striking the eardrum, can be told entirely in material terms, but the actual meaning of an utterance is non-physical: the same physical words spoken in French carry no meaning to someone who does not speak French, so meaning is an extra, incorporeal thing. Place, where a physical object is located, is similarly not made of stuff but is nonetheless there.

Critics in antiquity charged that linguistic meaning, void, time, and place make a random rag-bag thrown together to patch a materialist theory, and some see this as a weak point. The Stoics’ move was a metaphysical distinction: these incorporeals do not exist, but they are in some sense real; they subsist. O’Connor likens it to the medieval idea that things can exist in the intellect: a unicorn does not exist in the world, yet it is real in a way a four-sided triangle is not, because it can be coherently imagined. To fill out the picture, Sellers explains that the highest metaphysical category for the Stoics is designated by the Greek for “something,” reflecting their nominalism and rejection of Forms, since the broadest category is particularity. Beneath “something” sit two categories: bodies, which exist, and incorporeals, which subsist, and both are always particular, never universals slipping in through the back door. The convenience of saying that everything inconvenient is “real but does not exist” was not lost on the critics, and Sellers grants that much of the evidence here comes from hostile sources, with a little from Seneca, fuller theological material from Cicero’s On the Nature of the Gods, and a great deal from later Neoplatonist commentators on Aristotle writing polemically against the Stoa.

Ethics: From Self-Preservation to Virtue▶ 1:09:16

To understand Stoic ethics one must go back to the Athenians, since the Roman texts give the ethical worldview without its underpinning principles. A fairly full and sympathetic account survives in Cicero. The starting point is a simple observation: all animals, humans included, have a basic instinct for self-preservation. We pursue what helps us survive and avoid what harms us, food and water good, the big scary animal bad, and that is how we first ascribe value to things. This is a thoroughly naturalistic account of value, requiring no Platonic Form of the Good, and it fits the rest of the system.

As humans develop, however, we reach a point where we no longer see ourselves merely as animals fighting to survive but as rational agents. We come to value decency toward others, integrity, and rational consistency, and we begin to identify ourselves with that kind of character. Once we identify ourselves with our character, we realise that preserving that character is what our self-preservation truly consists in. Survival, for a rational agent, is no longer chiefly about feeding the body, though that remains a precondition, but about preserving the traits that make us who we are. This leads the Stoics to the conclusion that character is the single most important thing for living a good life.

The other things we first called good, food, shelter, money, health, are now reclassified. Only virtuous character is genuinely good, because only it guarantees our preservation as rational beings. The rest we still naturally pursue, but we no longer call them good; we call them preferred or dispreferred. Food, shelter, money, and health are preferred, their opposites dispreferred, but none is essential to living well or even, ultimately, to surviving as the kind of being we are. These external things are technically indifferents, things one should be indifferent to, though many Stoics still acknowledged that they preferred food to starvation.

A Broad Church: Indifferents and the Cynic Pull▶ 1:15:01

The category of preferred indifferents marks the distinctive break between Stoic and Cynic ethics. Both inherited the Socratic emphasis on virtuous character, but where the Cynics treated everything external as a matter of pure indifference, Zeno held that we still make choices and have preferences. This was contested from the start, with some early figures pushing back toward the more austere Cynic line.

Across more than five hundred years of Stoic tradition, the emphasis shifts. Panaetius, around the second to first century BC as Stoic ideas were taking root in Rome, stressed the importance of preferred things and moved away from the Cynic side. Epictetus, by contrast, the Stoic most read today, barely mentions the distinction at all; for him it is virtue all the way, with no need to worry about whether things are preferred or dispreferred. Stoicism was, in this sense, a broad church.

Is “Preferred” Just “Good” by Another Name?

A natural objection, raised in antiquity by critics such as Carneades, is that the Stoics are merely playing with words. If you still seek food and shelter but refuse to call them good only because it conflicts with your philosophy, perhaps you are just calling them good under another name, and saying nothing different from Aristotle, who held that external goods were genuinely useful for achieving virtue and eudaimonia. Cicero lays out exactly these objections in On Ends.

But Sellers locates a real difference through a concrete case. Suppose excellent health is good or preferred, and suppose you are struck down by a terrible illness. The Stoic says that if you keep the right frame of mind and character, you can still live a fully good, happy human life. The Aristotelian must say your life could be better if your health were restored. That is a substantial philosophical gap. For the Stoic, no matter what life throws at you, so long as you do not let it corrupt your frame of mind, you can live a full, rich human life, and adversity has not damaged what is most important. Your life could still be made worse, but only if you let yourself become bitter and resentful, not by the disability itself.

Why It Isn’t Self-Help

Here, at last, the modern dictionary sense of “stoic,” refusing to let circumstances disturb one’s mood, comes into view, but with a crucial correction. The Stoics are not offering self-help in the sense of a coping technique, a way to feel better about genuinely bad things. They claim, almost as a metaphysical principle, that external things literally do not affect what is good and bad. Letting an illness make your life less happy is not merely bad advice; it means you are mistaken about the nature of the world. There is nothing wrong with borrowing Stoic ideas to run a business or motivate yourself to exercise, but one should be aware that this is not what Stoicism is, and that applying these ideas to worldly success actually runs counter to its foundation, since part of the point is precisely that money has no inherent value. If you take up Stoic resilience in order to earn more, you have missed the point.

The full Stoic response to the chronically ill or disabled person is therefore stronger and stranger than consolation. You have made a mistake about where real value resides, the Stoic says: it lies not in physical health but in your mental state and character. Worse, by generating emotional turmoil you are actively damaging your rationality and your character, and so destroying the one thing where genuine goodness does reside. This is why the physics and logic matter and why Sellers begins with them: heard in isolation, the ethical advice sounds like a useful add-on to a life you already think is good, but understood properly, the ethics falls out of the metaphysics as a natural corollary rather than a comforting choice.

The Problem of Evil and the Two Faces of Fate▶ 1:24:22

The system generates its own version of the problem of evil. The Stoics believe the universe runs on a foundational rational principle, that it eternally recurs unchanged, which implies it is the best of all possible worlds, and yet Zeno is shipwrecked, people are disabled, and suffering abounds. The Stoics, while wanting to detach psychologically from the world’s sufferings, must, given their God and rational principle, account for that suffering, just as theists must.

Their answer, fittingly, sends an apparently ethical question back into the physics. Nature acts for nature’s benefit as a whole, not for the good or harm of its individual parts. There is a reason for everything that happens, but the reason for Zeno’s shipwreck has nothing to do with Zeno; it has to do with the weather patterns active at that moment. The animating principle, God or reason, is identified with providence, which evokes a best-of-all-possible-worlds picture, but also with fate, understood not as personal destiny but simply as cause and effect. The same order can be described as the divine will of Zeus or as a plain system of cause and effect that could not have gone otherwise: you were bound to fall ill given your genetic predisposition and environment, nature simply working itself out, with nothing you could do about it.

O’Connor presses that this becomes one of the strongest critiques of Stoicism, much as the problem of evil is the strongest argument against theism, since it is hard to account for apparently trivial suffering. One can say the shipwreck had to happen because of the wind, and that without it Zeno would never have founded Stoicism, but what about all the others who are shipwrecked and simply die? Sellers offers two further moves. The first depends on how personally we read this strange Stoic God: if it is a conscious agent, we land in familiar monotheistic debates, but if it is merely a rational principle organising matter, identified with cause and effect, it is not making decisions in the same way, and infuriatingly the sources describe God in both ways in different places. He adds a broader caution: the post-Enlightenment habit of sharply separating rational science from religion may simply not fit ancient Greek thought, which can be theological and materialistic at once, much as Western categories sit awkwardly on Daoism.

The second move is more radical: strictly speaking, the Stoic says, there is no evil happening at all. Losing a leg, or Zeno’s shipwreck, are merely dispreferred, not bad. The only genuinely evil thing would be the corruption of one’s own virtuous character. So there is no problem of evil because there is no evil.

Easy to Say: The Test of Practice

That is easy to say from a distance. Sellers points to a line, in fact from Seneca, a committed Stoic, acknowledging exactly this: someone observing that the Stoic consoles others expertly when they lose their children, yet would believe him more if he said the same about his own child. The real test of Stoicism is applying it to one’s own life, and neither speaker is sure he would stay serene if his own leg were cut off and told nothing bad had happened.

Yet the contrast is real. Some people who lose the use of a limb in an accident are destroyed by it; others, often ex-soldiers, go on to compete in the Paralympics, the trauma genuine but not slowing them down. Both reactions are understandable, but we instinctively call the second a form of strength and find it more enviable, which suggests an implicit recognition that one way of existing is genuinely better, not morally superior in the sense of deserving praise, but better to be. Given the choice between a pill that left you depressed and one that left you motivated and able to flourish, you would take the second; the Stoics claim that this better state is not just available but is the truth, and that recognising it lets you live a more flourishing life regardless of how many limbs you have. The choice lies in how we react to what happens to us, and that is where the power of the Stoic view lies, though, as Seneca insisted, none of us knows how we will react to an extreme situation until it happens, and only then is our strength of character truly revealed.

Seneca, or at least the broader Stoic tradition, made the point another way. You do not judge an architect by a lecture on architecture or a musician by an explanation of music theory; you judge the architect by the building and the musician by the performance. So too the philosopher is judged not by fine talk about the virtuous life but by living it when adversity comes. Musonius Rufus, a contemporary of Seneca who taught Epictetus and is well worth reading, made the same case with medicine: between a doctor expert at lecturing on theory and one experienced at actually stitching people up, you would choose the practitioner, even one who sounds confused about theory but has a perfect track record, over the eloquent one whose patients die.

Fate, Choice, and Compatibilism▶ 1:36:53

A final difficulty remains. If everything is determined by a material chain of cause and effect, and the universe recurs identically every time, with the same shipwreck always happening, how can anyone choose their response, as the Stoic ethics requires? Strictly speaking, given their determinism, the Stoics cannot believe in free will in the sense of an ability to do otherwise. Nevertheless, they argue, we still make our own choices: what we choose now follows from our present circumstances and from who we are. The web of fate works itself out to a single outcome, predetermined in a sense, though we do not know it in advance.

The important point is that we are genuine parties to the process. As one source puts it, fate does not do things to us; fate works through us. Confronted with, say, someone attacking an animal, one person intervenes and another shies away, and which one does which is determined by the kind of person they are, their past experiences, and the specifics of the situation. If we knew you were a committed member of the RSPCA, we could be highly confident you would step in, to the point of saying that walking away simply is not who you are, that you could not do it. This, Sellers and O’Connor agree, is exactly what academics call compatibilism.

This circles back to character and to the heart of virtue ethics. O’Connor offers the firefighter who runs into a burning building and afterwards says he did not even think, he simply had to, that he could not have lived with himself otherwise. In doing philosophy we worry that if people do not act freely they cannot act morally, yet in such cases we find the unhesitating act more virtuous, not less. That is the virtue in virtue ethics: not calculating the utility of each decision, but being the kind of person who simply behaves in certain ways, behaviour that is either more in line with nature and the truth about the world or more out of line with it, true or false action flowing necessarily from a true or false understanding of the world. Which brings everything back to character, and squarely into the realm of virtue ethics.
