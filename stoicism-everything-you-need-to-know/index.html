Was Socrates the Real Founder of Stoicism?

Alex O’Connor: Was Socrates the real founder of stoicism?

John Cellers: That’s a really good question. I think in many ways he was, or perhaps it’d be better to say he was the grandfather figure for Stoic ethics in particular. Obviously there are other bits of stoicism that I’m sure we’ll talk about a little later as well — physics and logic — but for ethics, I think yes, that’s a good way of putting it. He’s the first Greek philosopher to put on the table the idea that it’s a virtuous character, and only a virtuous character, that’s key to living a good life. That’s the famous Socratic idea that the Stoics then take up.

Alex O’Connor: But typically, if you ask somebody who was the founder of stoicism, they’ll tell you it’s this chap Zeno, right?

John Cellers: Yeah, that’s right.

Alex O’Connor: Zeno of Citium, who’s not the same Zeno famed for paradoxes.

John Cellers: That’s correct. There’s Zeno of Elea, who’s the paradox guy, who was a pupil of Parmenides. They were in southern Italy, so that’s much earlier. Zeno of Citium — Citium’s in Cyprus, so he comes from Cyprus, and he comes to Athens. There’s a great story that we have, which may or may not be true, which is that he was sent to Athens on a business trip by his father with a ship full of cargo. As he approached the harbor near Athens, there was a shipwreck and he lost everything. Where can I find people like him?” And at that moment another philosopher walks past, a guy who was a cynic. The bookseller says to him, “Follow that man. He’ll teach you what you want to know.” So that’s the origin story.

The Influence of the Cynics

Alex O’Connor: Zeno is the guy who then gets credited with founding what comes to be known as stoicism, and we’ll get into what stoicism actually is. But it’s interesting you say that he begins by following a cynic. The cynics are another sort of school of philosophers from the same time period — we’re talking ancient Greece here — where the most famous example of a cynic is Diogenes. These people would famously throw social custom to the wind. Diogenes is said to have lived in a barrel and to just urinate in the street, and when Alexander the Great comes to him and says, “Is there anything I can do for you?” he says, “Get out of my sun.” So this story begins with this recognition that a lot of the stuff in the world isn’t really relevant or meaningful, or it’s arbitrary to some degree. Do you think it’s fair to say that Zeno grows out of cynicism, is influenced by cynicism, or is this just a historical coincidence?

John Cellers: No, there’s definitely an influence, and it joins the dots for us with that connection with Socrates. Famously, Plato had described Diogenes the cynic as Socrates gone mad. So the thought is that what Diogenes was doing when he develops this cynic philosophy is taking Socratic ideas and pushing them to the extreme. Diogenes takes that idea that it’s a virtuous character that you need in order to live a good life, and only that, and then develops this attitude of complete indifference to literally everything else. As you say, he sleeps in a barrel, doesn’t care. At one point, one of our anecdotes suggests he was sold into slavery, and he’s like, “Well, what do I care? My virtuous character is still intact. Why would that bother me?”

So you get this extreme view in Diogenes and the subsequent cynics, where virtue is the only thing that matters to live a good life. “I’m not going to care about anything else that goes on in my life whatsoever.” As I say, that becomes very extreme. And part of that, connected to it as you hinted, is the idea that traditional social customs and conventions then become irrelevant. There’s a rejection of all those traditional conventions, and the idea that instead of living according to custom or law, you live according to nature — you’re just following nature’s prompts. Again, that’s a key cynic idea that the Stoics will take up.

In terms of what happens to Zeno, just to flesh things out: Zeno follows for a while, learns all about the cynic philosophy, and perhaps ultimately decides that’s not for him, even though it’s obviously a big influence on him. He then goes off to Plato’s Academy. Plato by this point is dead, but his followers are continuing the tradition, and we’re told that he studies at Plato’s Academy for ten years. That’s a really long time, so he learns all about Platonic philosophy at Plato’s Academy. He also studies with a couple of other guys who we now refer to as Megarians, who were another group of Socratic philosophers around at the time who had very strong interests in logic. But ultimately he decides he doesn’t want to be a cynic, he doesn’t want to be a Platonist, he doesn’t want to be a Megarian. So then he finally decides to set up under his own name, and that’s when he starts teaching at the Painted Stoa in the center of Athens. That’s when he starts the Stoic tradition, pulling elements from all of these other people that he studied with beforehand.

Alex O’Connor: That’s where we get this name stoicism, from the stoa where Zeno and his followers would meet to discuss philosophy.

Stoicism Is More Than an Ethical Philosophy

Alex O’Connor: Interestingly, we’re talking about this guy Zeno and we’re talking about the cynics, and a lot of people might think, okay, but I’ve heard of stoicism and I thought it was a Roman thing — like Marcus Aurelius and his meditations, and Seneca and guys like these. But I think we can tell a story here of what stoicism is and point out that it begins in Greece and comes in stages.

Another thing people might know about stoicism — and I’m framing this because people have asked me a lot to talk about stoicism, they say, “What do you think about stoicism?” and honestly I’ve always just said I know nothing about it, and that’s why I was so excited to learn about it and try to get some thoughts together so I could speak to you and do this as a bit of an introduction. The first thing to note is that in the popular imagination today, stoicism is viewed essentially as an ethical philosophy. To be a stoic is to live in a particular way, to be tolerant in the face of suffering and stuff like that. While that is true, the thing that people need to remember is that ideas like that don’t just spring up out of nowhere. Not only is there a historical story to tell, but stoicism is a broader philosophy that contains not just ethics, but also a really important tradition of logic and logical thinking, as well as physics or metaphysics relevant to the nature of the world and God’s existence — out of which the ethics are supposed to come.

So before diving into what stoicism actually is, can you give us a broad overview of the kind of three stages of stoicism and how it developed over time?

John Cellers: Yeah, sure. We start off with Zeno, as we’ve said, in Athens around 300 BC. That’s roughly when things kick off. There’s a tradition of people gathering together in Athens over a couple of centuries. Zeno’s immediate follower and successor was a chap called Cleanthes, who’s very interested in physics in particular — or we know that he had interesting things to say about physics. He was then followed by a chap called Chrysippus, and according to tradition Chrysippus is the most important of all of these early Stoics. He’s the guy that pulls together all the threads and really develops a substantial philosophical system. We’re told that he wrote 75 books, and they’re practically all lost. So there’s a huge amount that these early Stoics in Athens produced that we just don’t have anymore, and we’re just dealing with fragments and secondhand reports, trying to piece the thing together.

Chrysippus was most famous in antiquity as a logician. He’s arguably the second great logician in antiquity after Aristotle, and he made some really important contributions. So these early Stoics in Athens really are developing a rich, complex philosophical system. There’s metaphysics, physics, epistemology, logic, theories of grammar, as well as the ethics. It’s really rich and broad, and is comparable with what you would find in the Platonic or the Aristotelian traditions.

That tradition continues for quite a while, until probably the beginning of the 1st century BC. There’s a famous moment in Athenian history where a Roman general, Sulla, holds Athens under siege for a couple of years and a lot of things are destroyed. It’s no longer an attractive place to hang out. A lot of the philosophers run away — they go elsewhere. Some go to Rhodes, some go to Alexandria, some go to Rome and other places in Italy. That’s the point where this living tradition of Stoic philosophers in Athens, and philosophers in other schools as well to be honest, starts to die down a bit.

Just a few decades after that, we have the Roman statesman and philosopher Cicero write a whole series of philosophical books, and in those books he presents Stoic ideas to a Roman audience. He’s translating these ideas into Latin for the first time. That’s a key moment in the reception of stoicism and its introduction into the Roman world. Cicero’s philosophical works are really important for anyone interested in stoicism because most of those works by those earlier guys are now lost. So it’s Cicero’s accounts that are the earliest, fullest, and most complete accounts we have of early Stoic thought. Those become really important.

And then after Cicero, we get this whole series of other Romans that you mentioned who become really interested in Stoic ideas, like Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius. Those are the big three, the famous ones. The traditional view is that they’re far more interested in ethics, or they’re only interested in ethics, or they’re not even interested in ethical theory — they’re just interested in applied ethics, we might say. That’s the traditional story. I think we can complicate that. I think we can see ways in which they are in fact, if not doing interesting work in physics and metaphysics, at least engaging with those ideas. The reason why they’ve been so influential in shaping the subsequent image of what stoicism looks like, and the way people tend to think about it today, is that their works survive. You can go into your local bookstore and buy a copy of Marcus Aurelius’s meditations or Seneca’s letters and just read them. So unsurprisingly, lots of people who aren’t specialist academics think, okay, this is what stoicism is, without necessarily digging into the more scholarly literature that does the hard work of trying to reconstruct what the original early Athenian Stoics were about.

Alex O’Connor: And it’s not a bad thing. Most people interact with most philosophies in a relatively surface-level way. Maybe, yeah, I’ve heard of Aristotle, he’s the guy that thought virtue was important, and for most people that’s enough to get by on. But I do think that if you care about philosophy and you want to know what stoicism is, and you say, “Well, I think stoicism is this practical way of living, or this way of responding to suffering in the world or material conditions” — to say that that is what stoicism is, from what I’ve read, seems a bit like saying, if you asked somebody what Christianity is and they said, “Oh, it’s where you don’t have sex before marriage and you treat people as your brother.” That’s true, Christians do do that, but the whole Jesus’s resurrection and God’s existence is really important, and that ethical consequence is just one part of a much broader story. Similarly with stoicism, I don’t think it should be called an ethical philosophy, because there’s so much more going on. So the question is, what else is going on? Maybe we should start by delineating the different areas that Stoics were particularly interested in.

John Cellers: Yeah, absolutely. I agree 100% with what you’ve just said, and just to add a quick footnote to that. There’s a very traditional view which might say, okay, these early Athenian guys were the theoreticians that developed this complex system, and it’s all very tight and very complex, similar to which you might think about Aristotle’s philosophy. And then you’ve got these later Roman guys that are just interested in practical ethics, and if you’re a serious academic philosopher, they’re far less interesting. One of the things I would want to do is challenge both of those stereotypes. I would want to say that there’s much more continuity across the tradition. On the one hand, those earlier Athenian Stoics are doing all this complex theory, but they’re also interested in trying to find out how to live a good life and to live it themselves, drawing on that real existential motivation that they’ve inherited from Socrates and the cynics. And then our later Roman guys — the texts we have are talking about all these ethical ideas, but they’re reading the works of those early Athenian Stoics, they’re familiar with all those more complex theoretical ideas, and they’re trying to put them into practice. So really it’s the contingency of textual transmission that has given us this skewed view.

Alex O’Connor: That’s important, and it’s worth also noting that with so many ancient texts, if the text is lost, the way that we can ascertain what was in them is — sometimes we’re lucky enough to find fragments, and that occurs. But one of the most common places that we find quotations from texts that we’ve lost are in people who are responding to them and criticizing them. So we have access to what they said, but through the eyes, or through the writing, of their opponents. It’s a bit like if the only knowledge of the Bible that you had was quotes from The God Delusion by Richard Dawkins. You could probably piece together some of what the Bible was about, but you probably wouldn’t get the most Christian picture of what it was. I think we need to be aware of that when we’re talking about the earlier Stoics, that there’s a lot of uncertainty, but we can get a broad picture of the stuff that they cared about and the worldview that they attested to.

Stoic Logic and Chrysippus

Alex O’Connor: I don’t know what you consider to be the foundational starting point. I think most people start — and you start your book on stoicism — with logic, rather than jumping in with this ethical stuff. It’s almost like you could just shelve that and forget it for a moment and start with the foundation of philosophy, which is logic: how arguments are constructed, how words are used, how we ascertain truth. And the Stoics had their own unique approach to this, right?

John Cellers: Yes, that’s right. As I mentioned earlier, Chrysippus is famous as a logician. He develops his own system of formal logical argument. He effectively develops what we now call Stoic syllogistic. Aristotelian syllogisms are of the form: all humans are animals, all animals are mortal, so all humans are mortal. All A’s are B, all B’s are C, so all A’s are C. Chrysippus was interested in different kinds of arguments — something along the lines of, if it’s raining, I’ll take my umbrella; it’s raining, so I’ll take my umbrella. If P, then Q; P; therefore Q. A very different type of argument. As it happens, that Stoic type of argumentation is the sort that was resurrected much later by Frege, and has much more in common with modern formal logic. So he was very much ahead of his time.

Alex O’Connor: The Aristotelian logical system dominated in medieval philosophy, but the modern celebrated logic of the mathematization is more indebted to the Stoics. Just to be clear, because of what you just said, people might be listening and go, “They sound kind of similar.” When you give an Aristotelian syllogism — and a syllogism is just an argument of premise, premise, conclusion — it’s something like, all A’s are B, A is C, therefore all B’s are C, or whatever. You’re using terms like “all,” or you might say, no dogs have three legs, Johnny has three legs, therefore Johnny is not a dog — some or all. And you’re talking about terms, so the A and the B in the argument will be like, all A’s are B, all cats are animals. The things you’re inserting are terms like cat, animal. Whereas the Stoics — the things they’re inserting, or representing with those letters, are not terms but entire propositions: if P then Q, if it is raining then I will take my umbrella. So in Aristotelian logic the things we’re dealing with are things like cat, dog, human; in Stoic logic we’re dealing with things like “it is raining,” “the sky is blue,” “I will get wet,” that kind of stuff. Those things sound different, but people might say, what’s the important difference between thinking logically in terms of propositions versus terms, as Aristotle did?

John Cellers: I don’t think we need to see them as being in competition with one another. Obviously they’re both useful systems to have. In the Middle Ages, Aristotelian logic dominates, and there’s a sense of, okay, this is just what logic looks like. Whereas what Chrysippus had already done — although for the most part it was largely forgotten — was say, well, actually there are other ways of thinking about how arguments function. There’s more to rationality than just “all” and “some.” There are other ways we can think about consequences of certain claims. So he could see that there was more work to be done in developing logic. That propositional logic has been very fruitful. Some of his logical ideas we see taken up in the Middle Ages by the very famous philosopher and logician Peter Abelard. It’s not entirely clear whether he had access to some text that gave him some hint about what Stoic logic was all about, or whether he simply managed to develop it again himself, because he was such an able logician. And then we see it taken up more recently by Frege. So it’s become a really important part of the logical tradition, but it has its origins with the Stoics.

Alex O’Connor: You could also think of this Aristotelian logic not just in terms of the things that are replaced by the letters, but Aristotelian logic will have this language of “all” or “some,” or “every” or “no” — like no dogs have three legs — whereas the language of Stoic logic is more like “if, then.” It’s conditional claims, showing what follows from other things, but putting that into the premises. So it’s just two different ways of thinking about the world. But this is the foundational starting point for philosophy, right? We’ve got this logical system, we’re thinking in terms of propositions and how they follow from each other. Okay, but the Stoics have that — what do they build on?

Logic, Empiricism, and Where Knowledge Comes From

John Cellers: Let me roll back a bit. The Stoics divide philosophy into three broad areas: logic, physics, and ethics. We hear accounts of the way in which different Stoics proposed teaching those three parts in different orders. They didn’t quite make up their mind whether one was foundational or not. Some of them would have started teaching physics first and then logic later. So I think it would be a mistake to think that logic is the foundational bit. I think we’ve got this really complex, interdependent system where ultimately you need all three bits, and what order you learn them in is an aside.

Alex O’Connor: Is it broadly agreed that the ethics comes last, even if we don’t know what comes first, or did some people maybe even start with the ethics?

John Cellers: No, I think at least one of the Stoics put physics last, and the highest bit of physics would have been theology. So that would have been the grand finale in terms of someone’s education. That would be the first thing to say: logic isn’t necessarily the ultimate foundation, but it’s a central piece for sure. The other thing to say is that when they’re talking about logic, they mean something much broader than just the formal logical argumentation we’ve just been talking about. Logic includes everything to do with logos. The Greek word logos means reason, argument — it means word, so language more broadly. And within logic broadly conceived, they would include epistemology, theories of knowledge, where we get our ideas from. The Stoics are empiricists.

So the foundational claim in Stoic logic is that we’re empiricists. We gain knowledge through the senses, we gain information through our senses, and we receive that in our minds. The Stoics think that we then translate that into propositions. You see a cat sitting on the mat. Your mind is presented with this proposition: the cat is on the mat. And then you decide whether to assent to that proposition or not. You either say yes or no — I believe it, or I don’t believe it. If you say “yes, I believe that,” then that creates the belief in your mind that the cat is on the mat. And of course, if that belief is true, then you have knowledge. The formal logic that we were just talking about is constructed out of all these propositions that are in your head. So: knowledge through the senses first, you assent to or reject the information you receive, that creates these beliefs in the form of propositions, and then you start constructing your logical arguments off the back of it. That’s what logic’s concerned about. If your senses are reliable, and you assent correctly and don’t make mistakes, and then your arguments are valid, you’re going to end up with knowledge out the other end of this process.

Alex O’Connor: So the Stoics believe in empiricism. They think that all knowledge ultimately comes from the input that we get from the world through our senses, our eyes and our ears and things like that — which sounds sensible. People will say, well, I’ve heard of that view of empiricism, someone like David Hume might come to mind. Some people, if I’m not mistaken, think that the Stoics anticipate this Humean view of the world. The Stoics weren’t too dissimilar, at least in their epistemology here, right?

John Cellers: Yes, that’s true. The other famous early modern empiricist is John Locke. In John Locke’s famous essay, he described the mind at birth as like a blank sheet of paper on which experience writes our beliefs. And we have information that the Stoics also had already said that at birth the mind is like a blank sheet of paper on which impressions stamp themselves. So that core modern empiricist idea is already in stoicism, absolutely 100%. One corollary of this is that if all of our knowledge comes through the senses and the mind is a blank sheet of paper, then there are no innate ideas. There’s no pre-existing information that we have already. That’s obviously a challenge to the Platonic idea that we have some kind of innate knowledge that we simply recollect and bring back to our minds through experience.

How the Stoics Handle Universals: Nominalism

Alex O’Connor: Yes, and the Stoics position themselves against Plato’s views in some important ways. One of the things that Plato is most famous for is his dealing with universals. I’m sat on a chair and you’re sat on a chair. These are not the same chair — yours is over there, mine’s over here, they’re in different positions, made out of different physical material. And yet they’re both chairs. So there’s something they’ve got in common that they share, and yet they’re totally separate things. Plato is like, well, that’s because there’s this form of chairness in which they both participate. The physical world is just an imperfect reflection of this perfect realm of forms, where there’s the form of a chair and the form of the good and the form of justice. So this is a way of saying that individual chairs participate in this universal thing called chairness. For Plato, that means there is this realm of forms, and when you interact with the world you’re drawing upon that other realm. And for Plato, weirdly, it’s also got this memory thing — you’re bringing it back from your premortal memory of the realm of forms. The Stoics don’t agree with Plato, as we’ve just said. But then the Stoics are confronted with the same question. There’s a chair here, there’s a chair there, they seem to have something in common. But if all we have are our empirical observations of something over there and something over here, how do the Stoics deal with this problem of universal chairness?

John Cellers: Just like modern empiricists, they’re going to argue that the concept of “chair” that we have is something we’ve constructed on the basis of experiencing lots of things that have certain similarities. But the concept is simply something we’ve constructed after the event, after experience. It’s not something that’s come beforehand.

Alex O’Connor: Plato is effectively saying, well, you wouldn’t even be able to recognize these two things as chairs unless you already had the concept of chair in some sense in your head. That’s the view that the Stoics would want to challenge — no, no, no, we develop these concepts as we go. They’re ultimately the product of convention. We take objects in the world that are material things arranged in different ways, and we put labels on them: call that a table and that a chair. There’s no real category called tables and chairs — that’s just something I’ve invented. I’ve talked about it in the context of mereology and called that mereological nihilism, but another good word for this is nominalism: to say that that is a table in name only. There’s not a real category of table, it’s just a name that I’ve given it. In the modern world we call that nominalism. Do you think it’s therefore fair to say that the Stoics are one of our earliest schools of nominalism?

John Cellers: Yeah, I think that is fair to say. I’d perhaps add that a couple of figures just before them — interestingly, Diogenes the cynic, the guy who is supposedly only interested in flamboyant ethics — also challenged Plato on some of these issues. He sort of said, well, you talk about tablehood, where the hell is this? I’ve never seen this. I can see tables, I can’t see the idea of table. So you get the sense that perhaps the cynics were in this place as well, also challenging Plato on some of these ideas. Maybe that’s also part of the cynic inheritance that stoicism takes up. But stoicism, so far as the evidence we have, really develops and runs with it and makes it into part of a much more consistent, fully developed theory.

How the Stoics Differ From Aristotle on Forms

Alex O’Connor: Another person who disagreed with Plato about forms and the existence of universals is Aristotle. People are probably more familiar with the idea that Plato follows Socrates, Aristotle comes after Plato, and they disagree with and improve upon each other. Aristotle also disagrees with the existence of forms in the way that Plato had them, but in a different way to the Stoics, right? They deal with the problem differently.

John Cellers: Yes. Aristotle does think that there are things as forms, which I think is the sort of thing that modern philosophers would call natural kinds. We were talking about chairs and tables, which are just artificial, but if we’re talking about, say, different species of animals — which is very much what Aristotle was interested in — then it’s like, okay, there are birds over here, there are horses over here. This isn’t just us arbitrarily coming up with labels and making distinctions. The distinction between these different types of creatures is a fact of nature. In that sense there’s a form of horse, and you can go and look at individual horses and point at them, and you can see the form of horse. It’s right there. It doesn’t exist independently of those individual horses, but there’s clearly a shared set of properties that all these horses have, which is beyond just how we are choosing to think about it.

That seems like a really sensible thing for Aristotle to say. He’s a really smart guy, I’m a huge fan of Aristotle too. But the Stoics don’t go down that route so much. They don’t want to talk about forms in precisely that sense, although they do talk about objects in the natural world having certain qualities. This is where it gets really quite complex and difficult to piece it all together. They acknowledge that there are qualities that objects will have, but they’ll resist talking about forms having any existence beyond the particulars. So there’s a certain amount of common ground with Aristotle there, in that we’re interested in particular things existing. And of course, like Aristotle, they’re empiricists. In that sense both Aristotle and the Stoics can seem surprisingly modern compared to the popular view of Plato, which looks quite different.

Stoic Metaphysics: Matter and Breath

Alex O’Connor: We’ve talked about how knowledge comes into our minds according to the Stoics, who’ve got this empiricism, and we’ve talked a little bit about logic, what they do with the resulting information. But let’s talk about the actual nature of the world. Let’s talk about their metaphysics. This is where we move from talking about logic, or logos — which, as you say, encompasses more than just propositional logic, the epistemology stuff as well. On top of that, we might want to build a picture of what the world actually looks like. We can start by saying that the Stoics are materialists about the world. They don’t believe in anything except for physical matter, right? How would they cash out what that means?

John Cellers: Yes. Famously they say only bodies exist — physical things in the world. And connecting up with what we were just saying about nominalism, they also say only particulars exist: no universals, no Platonic forms. Particular individual physical objects — that’s the architecture of the world, that’s what’s real. In that sense we would naturally describe them as materialists, so that’s the basic foundational idea. But they over-complicate things — well, I say over-complicate things. Just as Aristotle had talked about physical objects being a mixture of what he called matter and form — there’s the horse, it has the form of a horse, it has the shape, the structure, and there’s the matter that it’s made out of, so the horse is a combination of matter and form, this is his hylomorphism — the Stoics also think that everything in the world is a combination of two things. One they will call matter, and the other, this is where it gets really difficult, they call pneuma, which is breath, or spirit as it becomes later in later tradition. Let’s call it breath. So they think everything is a combination of matter and breath. They say that matter is the passive element and breath is the active element, and every physical object in the world is a combination of these two things.

Superficially that might sound a bit like what Aristotle is saying — there’s the form that gives something its structure and identity, and then there’s the matter that’s just the stuff it’s made out of. The Stoics are saying the same thing but with one really important difference, which is that the pneuma, or breath, they think is another material component. These are both material principles that in some way are mixed together: a passive matter, but with some kind of material active principle permeating it, which is giving it its structure, or in the case of living beings is making them to be alive. In the case of a human being, we are a body made out of matter, but our entire body is permeated with this breath, which they identify with our soul, which is the thing that makes us alive.

Alex O’Connor: But do they also view chairs and tables in that way, as having this breath? If so, it might make sense, because we’ve got to be careful with our terms here since we’re translating. John Cellers: Yeah, it’s going to be in the table for sure, in all inanimate objects. It’s going to be the thing that gives the table its structure, its hardness, its qualities. It’s the thing that holds it together — so that we’re not just looking at a pile of dust. The most basic level of tension gives cohesion to the table or the chair. A next higher level of tension will give plants their principle of life, and then in animals it will give the principle of perception and movement, and then in human beings it will give rationality. In each case, what we’re seeing is an increase in tension. This is often referred to as the scale of nature. There’s no radical difference in kind between any of these different things. There’s no difference in kind between the human rational mind and an animal mind, or even what’s going on in a plant. As one scholar has put it, what we’re really thinking about here is different levels of complexity. The breath is what is giving objects the complex organization that makes them what they are.

Stoic Cosmology: The World’s Life Cycle and Eternal Recurrence

There were a lot of debates in antiquity, as there still are today, about whether the universe, as we might say today, had a beginning, whether it’s infinite, whether it’s finite, whether it’s got edges. The Stoics had some pretty specific views on this, right?

John Cellers: Yeah, this is where it starts to get slightly weird. This is the stuff that the modern fans of stoicism will have no interest in whatsoever, but it is part of the theory.

Alex O’Connor: Notice how far away we are from the sort of “if you lose your possessions then you can control your own emotions.” I hope this is becoming clear to people, that stoicism is a lot more than just that.

John Cellers: Yeah. We’ve got nature identified with God, a living being. So nature is alive, and like any other living being, nature will have its life cycle. This is where it starts to get crazy. The Stoic view is that the natural world is in effect born at a certain moment, it has its life cycle, and at the end of this life cycle it is then destroyed. The moment of birth and the moment of destruction is characterized as all of the passive matter being consumed by the active principle — identified with reason, identified with God, identified with breath, but also identified with fire. This is an idea that they take from the earlier Greek philosopher Heraclitus. They suggest that the entire world is consumed in fire — a moment of singularity. Everything becomes pure active principle, so everything becomes pure God, and then it’s reborn, kind of like a phoenix, and then God has another life cycle.

As I say, some of these ideas may have come from Heraclitus: the idea that there’s logos animating nature, the idea that fire is a key principle. So God has this life cycle, and each cycle, they say, is identical. Again, the idea of eternal return, eternal recurrence, that is often associated with Nietzsche. The Stoics never use that phrase, but they effectively have that theory. If the world is organized rationally by this divine principle, then what he’s going to produce is going to be perfect. So why would he change it the second time round? You’re going to get exactly the same cycle the second time, third time, and so on. So you have cyclical time and endless repetition of exactly the same events. And again, the Stoics are interested in the logical puzzles: is Socrates in cycle one the same as Socrates in cycle two, and the same as Socrates in cycle three? If it’s exactly the same life that you go through, can you even distinguish between individual cycles if they’re identical? All those sorts of philosophical problems are then discussed in the literature.

Stoic Ethics: From Self-Preservation to Virtuous Character

Alex O’Connor: I think it’s time to do it: the ethics. A lot of this stuff is from hostile sources and whatnot, but, like you said, the majority of the texts which we still have today are from this later Stoa, or Roman Stoa, which has come to be associated with ethics. Maybe that is just a historical contingency — maybe they wrote and read all about physics and logic, and it just didn’t survive. But nonetheless, today popularly stoicism is understood as an ethical philosophy. So I want to ask you, what is the ethical worldview of the Stoics? And more specifically, now we can probably answer the question of how we get from this view of the world and the universe and the eternal-fire-recreation stuff to quite particular ethical doctrines. What’s that story?

John Cellers: Yes. It’s also worth saying that if you just read the works of the Roman Stoics, you’ll get a certain ethical worldview, but you won’t get the fundamental underpinning principles. So if you really want to understand Stoic ethics, you’ve also got to go back to our Athenians. In this case we’ve got a fairly full account of early Stoic ethics that Cicero preserves for us. Although Cicero wasn’t a Stoic, he’s reasonably sympathetic towards Stoic ethics, so he gives a fairly full and fairly friendly account, we might say.

We talked a bit earlier about Socrates and the idea that it’s virtue, or character, that’s the most important thing for living a good life. In order to understand how the Stoics get to that, I’ll just set out the basic principles. The starting point is the very simple idea, the very simple observation, that all animals including humans have a basic instinct to self-preservation. We’re all just trying to survive, and we will pursue things that we think will help us survive, and we will avoid things that we think will harm us. Food and water, good; big scary animal, bad. Simple as that. And that’s how we ascribe value to things, the Stoics think — a completely naturalistic way of thinking about value. You don’t need a Platonic ideal form of the good. Food and water, good, that protects me; scary animal, bad. So we have a naturalistic account of values, which fits with what we’ve already been saying about the metaphysics and the logic.

But as we develop as human beings, we reach a certain point where we come to see ourselves not merely as animals fighting for survival. We see ourselves as rational agents, and we have certain sets of values, and we start to value things like being decent towards other people, having integrity, or being rationally consistent in our beliefs. We start to identify ourselves as that type of person: I’m the sort of person who’s rationally consistent, who acts with integrity, who behaves well towards other people around me. If I start to identify myself with that kind of character, I’ll come to realize it’s the preservation of that character that is what my self-preservation actually looks like. My survival is not based on whether I can feed this physical body. Although that’s obviously still a precondition, my survival as a rational agent is based on me preserving all of those character traits that make me who I am and make me a human being. So that leads the Stoics to this thought that it’s our character that is the single most important thing for living a good life. That’s the thing that we want to preserve.

And then they’ll say that all of the other things that we initially valued, like food and shelter, we initially called good. But now we’re going to say it’s our virtuous character that’s the only thing that’s really good. That’s the thing that enables us to live a good life, the thing that guarantees our preservation as a rational human being. All those other things are things that we still naturally pursue, but we’re not going to call them good anymore. We’re going to call them preferred or dispreferred. Food, shelter, money, health — these are all preferred things. Of course we all want them, we have this natural instinct to pursue them, and their opposites are going to be dispreferred. But they’re not so essential for us to living a good life, or even to surviving. What really matters is developing this rational character.

Preferred and Dispreferred Indifferents

Alex O’Connor: That in particular was a bit of a controversy in early stoicism, right? You’ve got this realization that the thing that really makes you you is your character, and that your physical self is secondary to that — the most important thing about you is who you are as a person, and virtue will get you there. So all of this other stuff like food and shelter, although we kind of want to say it’s good, it’s not really good. The early Stoic position on this is that they are so-called indifferents — like, “indifference” with a “ce,” but with a “ts,” like they are indifferent things you should be indifferent to. And yet some Stoics were like, okay, yeah, they don’t really matter, we’re not going to call them good, but I do prefer food to starvation. Although, as you’ve told us, we inherit this view of stoicism that there are preferred and dispreferred indifferents, there’s another way this could have gone, because there were some people who thought that there shouldn’t be this concept of preferred and dispreferred, right?

John Cellers: Yes. We mentioned much earlier that Zeno spent time studying with the cynics, and so he inherits this Socratic idea from the cynics: it’s all about virtuous character. As we said, Diogenes and Crates and the other cynics take this idea of an attitude of indifference towards the external world to an extreme view where you don’t care about anything whatsoever. So the introduction of this distinction between preferred and dispreferred looks like it’s a key distinctive Stoic idea in particular, that Zeno introduces, that differentiates Stoic ethics from cynic ethics. The cynics will say everything external is just a matter of indifference, and Zeno will say, no, we’re still going to have choices and make preferences. As you say, there are some early figures at that time who want to push back against that and say, no, no, no, we prefer the more cynic view.

What’s interesting, if you track through the Stoic tradition, which lasts over 500 years, is that you’ll find different people place different emphasis on this idea. A very famous writer a bit later called Panaetius, who was in the second-to-first century BC, at the time when these ideas were taking off in Rome, really stresses the importance of things being preferred and moves away from the more cynic side of things, we might say. A little later, someone like Epictetus — a very famous Stoic that lots of people read today — barely mentions this distinction at all. Quite strikingly, I’ve just said that this is the defining idea in Stoic ethics that distinguishes it from the cynics, and it rarely appears in Epictetus. With Epictetus it’s virtue, virtue all the way — just focus on virtue, that’s all that matters; you don’t need to worry about whether things are preferred or dispreferred. So there’s almost a range of views within what’s quite a broad church of Stoic ethical thinking.

Alex O’Connor: But there’s also maybe this thought: okay, so the Stoics want to say that none of this external stuff really matters — none of this non-character-related stuff really matters — and then they go, “But I am a bit hungry.” “No, no, no, that’s not good, that’s just preferred.” Can’t someone listening to that go, but you’re still seeking food, you’re still seeking shelter, and okay, you don’t want to call them good because that conflicts with your philosophy, but maybe you just should? Maybe you’re just calling them good by other names. Aristotle thought that virtue was the most important thing, but he also did think that these external things were good — they were useful and worthwhile in achieving virtue and getting to eudaimonia, the ultimate state of happiness for the ancient Greeks. If I’m not mistaken, some early critics of stoicism, like Carneades and a few other people, were criticizing the Stoics for saying, you’re kind of just saying the same thing as Aristotle. Is that fair? Because it sounds to me a little bit like — I’m all on board with these Stoic ideas, but when they say food isn’t good but it is preferable, I’m like, what’s the difference?

John Cellers: Yeah, and you’re absolutely right. People did raise that objection in antiquity. Again, Cicero, in one of his books, On Ends, outlines all of this and raises precisely these sorts of objections: are you just playing with words? Are you just varying terminology, but is the basic idea identical? There’s one line of argument there which suggests that the Stoics and the Aristotelians are basically saying the same thing, just using different vocabulary. But the key issue is this. Let’s say that it is preferred, or good, for you to be in excellent health, and let’s say that you’re struck down with some horrible illness. Now, a Stoic is going to say that if you’ve still got the right frame of mind and the right character, you can still live a good, happy human life. An Aristotelian is going to say, well, you’re not — your life could be better if your health were restored. That feels like a big philosophical difference. If someone’s struck down by disability, an Aristotelian would have to say it could be better, whereas the Stoic is going to say, no, you can still live as good a human life as anyone else, so long as your character remains.

Alex O’Connor: Yeah, your life might be worse, but not because of the disability.

John Cellers: Well, your life could be worse if you become bitter and resentful about the fact that this thing has happened, but so long as you don’t let that happen. So there’s a sense in which there’s something quite positive about what the Stoics are saying — that no matter what life throws at you, no matter what happens to you, so long as you don’t let that adversely affect your frame of mind, you can still live a full, rich human life. You don’t have to let those things have in some way damaged or destroyed what’s most important.

Why Stoicism Isn’t Just Self-Help

Alex O’Connor: Now it feels like we’re finally approximating what people know stoicism to be. If you look in a dictionary for the word stoic, it’s like, oh, this person’s being very stoic about it — it means that kind of thing where they’re saying, I’m not going to allow this to upset me, or change my mood, or change my happiness. But hopefully this can clear up what might be a misconception, and a subtle one, which is that the Stoics aren’t offering you self-help in the sense of saying, yeah, I know it sucks when this happens to you, but here’s a way to feel better, here’s a way to deal with the fact that this stuff’s really bad and here’s how to get over it. They’re not just doing that because, oh, it just feels better if we can ignore that stuff or get over it. They’re saying literally it does not affect what’s good and bad. Not that, oh, you should treat it that way because it will make you feel better, but literally, almost as a metaphysical principle: if you’re allowing illness to make you have a less happy life, it’s not just that that’s bad advice, it’s that you’re actually mistaken about the nature of the world.

So the modern popular understanding of stoicism that comes up in the self-help context — nothing wrong with it. If you want to borrow some Stoic ideas and use them to apply to running a business, or motivating yourself to be healthy and get in the gym, then great. But you should probably be aware that that’s not what stoicism is. In fact, by applying these Stoic ideas to those worldly things, you’re kind of running counter to the foundation of what stoicism is about at its most fundamental level.

John Cellers: Yeah, that’s a really interesting issue, and you can feel it coming up quite a lot at the moment. If you think that you’re going to take Stoic ideas about resilience and fortitude in order to make you more successful and earn more money, then you’ve missed the point, because part of what they’re saying is precisely that money has no inherent value. So in that sense, yes, people could miss the point. And again, you’re absolutely right — they’re not saying these things simply because they think it might make you feel better. They’re saying it because they think it reflects facts about the world.

Alex O’Connor: So the person who might find themselves disabled or chronically ill, if they start saying “this is terrible, my life has been ruined,” the Stoic will come along and say, well, no, you’re making a mistake. You fail to understand where real value resides, and it doesn’t reside in physical health, it resides in your mental state. Not only have you made a mistake, you’ve not understood our value theory, but also you are actively damaging yourself by creating emotional turmoil in your head, which is damaging your rationality and damaging your character. So you’re destroying the most important thing there is, the thing where genuine goodness does reside.

Alex O’Connor: This is why it’s important to understand the physics and the logic and these views of the Stoics, and why I think it’s good to start with them. If you just hear about these ethical ideas — stuff that happens external to you shouldn’t affect your mood or your character — you might, in isolation, go, “Yeah, that sounds pretty good. I reckon if I adopted that view, that would be good for me,” because you presuppose that you’ve got this thing that you think is good in the world. You want to be happy and content and live a nice life, and that seems like a useful thing to plug into my worldview. That’s fine, but the Stoics are being much stronger than that. When you understand their metaphysical view, the ethics kind of falls out of that. They haven’t just chosen it because it seems nicer to them; it’s a natural corollary of these beliefs about the nature of the world.

The Problem of Evil for the Stoics

Alex O’Connor: Having said that, the Stoics believe that the universe abides by a foundational rational principle. They believe in God. They believe that there is this eternally recurring universe, which implies it’s the best of all possible worlds, because it can’t be improved upon, and that’s why it keeps occurring in the same way. And yet Zeno gets shipwrecked, and yet people are struck down with disability, and yet there’s all kinds of suffering that we have to deal with in the world. So the Stoics, whilst wanting to psychologically detach themselves from the sufferings of the world, given their metaphysics about God and the rational principle, have to account for that classical problem for religious people — the problem of evil. That’s also a problem for Stoics, right? I wonder if you can give us an indication of if and how they might have dealt with that.

John Cellers: Yes, we do find Stoics touch on that problem in particular. Again, it’s the thought that nature is doing what nature does for nature’s best interest. It’s concerned with what benefits nature as a whole, not with what is good or bad for the individual parts. So there’s a sense in which there is a reason for anything that happens. There’s a reason for Zeno’s shipwreck, but it’s got nothing to do with Zeno — it’s to do with the order of whatever weather patterns were active at that moment.

Here’s perhaps another interesting thought to put on the table, and again it shows us the interconnectedness of the Stoic system. In order to answer this question, which feels like a very ethical question, we’re going to have to go back to the physics. This animating principle within nature — God, reason, however you want to call it — the Stoics will identify this with providence, which makes it sound as if we’re in the best-of-all-possible-worlds territory that you were mentioning. But they also identify it with fate. And again, not fate in the sense of “this is your destiny,” but fate simply in the sense of cause and effect. There’s a logic and a reason to the way the world works, and you could describe that in terms of the divine will of Zeus, but equally you could describe it in terms of, look, there’s just a logical system of cause and effect, this is just the way the world works, it couldn’t happen any other way. You were bound to get struck down with this horrible disease because of your genetic predisposition and the environment you live in. So it was destined to happen — that’s just nature working itself out, and there’s nothing you can do about that.

Alex O’Connor: I think that so long as the Stoic must believe in this almost perfect rationality of the universe, that it can’t be improved upon, this becomes one of the strongest critiques of stoicism, in the same way that the problem of evil is the biggest atheist argument that exists for most religious traditions. It’s very difficult to account for what seem to be trivial forms of suffering. You could say, well, that shipwreck had to happen because of the conditions of the wind, and then you want to say, but why were the conditions of the wind like that? Well, maybe because if that hadn’t happened to Zeno, then he never would have founded stoicism. Okay, but what about all the people who have a shipwreck and then die? What about them? These questions are really difficult to answer, and I just wanted to note that they’re present for stoicism, but they tend to answer them in similar ways to how someone who believes in the existence of the triomni God who created the universe would answer as to how he would be compatible with evil as well. It’s a similar conversation that happens in some texts.

John Cellers: Yes, that’s certainly true. I suppose it depends how much we want to describe this very strange, weird Stoic God in personalist terms. If we think of this Stoic God as some kind of conscious agent, then we fall into that territory where it looks very much like the sorts of debates we get in monotheistic religion. But if we think of this Stoic God as simply this rational principle that organizes matter, which is simply identified with cause and effect, that doesn’t sound as if it’s something that is making decisions in quite the same way. And here’s the really infuriating, frustrating bit: in our sources we will find the Stoic God described in both of those ways in different places. It’s incredibly difficult to really pin down, okay, precisely what did they mean by this, or are we bringing a modern set of categories and imposing them on this?

Just as an aside, we tend to think of rational science, and then religion, and think of those two things as very distinct. This is a kind of Enlightenment inheritance. When people talk about, say, Chinese philosophy, and they talk about, I don’t know, Daoism, and they say, okay, what’s going on here? Is this a kind of natural philosophy? Is this religion? How do these categories that we’re operating with really work? Some commentators will say, well, maybe these Western categories don’t really fit with this Eastern way of thinking. There’s a sense in which you could almost say the same about ancient Greek thinking. These post-Enlightenment categories — is that the right way to think about what these ancient Greeks are thinking about? How can this be theological and materialistic at the same time?

Just to come back to the problem of evil, a couple of other thoughts would be: one, in these situations when we’re talking about evil happening, strictly speaking the Stoics are going to say there is no evil happening. Nothing bad’s happened. Someone cuts your leg off, Zeno’s in his shipwreck — these are just dispreferred, they’re not bad. So there is no evil, so there is no problem of evil. The only genuinely evil thing would be if your virtuous character was corrupted.

Knowing the Theory Versus Living It

Alex O’Connor: But, you know, easy for us to say. In fact, I can’t remember who it is — you quote them in your book, it’s one of these early writers on stoicism. Or maybe it’s not. You said, who’s the Roman that writes about stoicism but isn’t a Stoic?

John Cellers: Cicero.

Alex O’Connor: Maybe it’s Cicero, where he’s saying — it’s someone who says, you know, when I’ve seen you expertly console other people because they’ve lost their children, I think, wow, that’s compelling, the things you’re saying. But I’d believe you more if you were saying it about your own child. It’s easy to say this kind of stuff, but the real way to prove the truth and efficacy of stoicism is to apply it to your own life. I sort of imagine, if my leg got cut off and you were sat there going, “You know, Alex, just dispreferred, nothing bad has happened,” I’d be there like, I’m not sure I’m convinced by that.

John Cellers: Now, interestingly, it was Seneca who said that. So it was a committed Stoic who said that, who’s kind of saying, look, I know the theory, but actually walking the walk, actually putting this into practice and really following through — this is hard. So he’s acknowledging how challenging some of these views are. But with the cut-off-leg example, you can think of people who — you may even know people who have had debilitating accidents. Someone who’s been in a terrible car accident and has lost the use of a leg, and it really hits them hard and really destroys them. And then you see other people, often ex-soldiers who lose a leg, who are off in the Paralympics doing absolutely incredible things. No doubt it was a horrific, traumatic experience to go through, but it’s not slowing them down, it’s not dampened their spirits, and they’re just getting on with things. So we can see ways in which different people might respond differently to the same material.

Alex O’Connor: And there’s a sense in which both of those reactions are perfectly understandable. Of course, I can’t imagine what such a thing would be like. However, when we look at those two reactions, we want to say of the latter one, that takes real strength, or you’d be more envious of that person than you would be of the other person. You’d describe it as a form of strength. But if you think about what that means — to be strong means to carry a load that you need to carry — there’s this implicit understanding that there is something kind of better and not better. Not better like morally, like we should judge them, “Oh, they’ve done a better job,” but just like that is a more preferable thing to be. And not in the sense of preferred indifferents, but a genuinely better way to exist. If I gave you the option, after you lose a leg, you can take a pill, and that pill is either going to make you super depressed about it, or you can take a different pill that will make you really motivated and get on with your life and still be happy — you take the second. I guess the Stoics are saying that not only is that available to you as an option, that is just the truth. If you can only recognize that that’s the truth, you’ll be living a more flourishing life regardless of your number of limbs.

John Cellers: Yeah, absolutely. That’s a choice that we make, how we react to the things that happen to us. That’s where the power of the Stoic view comes along. But the point that Seneca is making, of course, is that neither of us know how we’re going to react to that extreme situation until it happens to us. Only when it actually happens to us will our strength of character truly be revealed. That’s a really terrifying, sobering thought, but it’s also the truth.

Alex O’Connor: Exactly.

John Cellers: There’s a very real sense that you learn from experience. You’re put through trials and tests, and along the way you learn what kind of character you really have.

Alex O’Connor: Was it Seneca who then went on to say that you don’t judge the architect by his ability to come and deliver a really interesting lecture about architecture? You don’t judge the musician by his ability to stand and explain music theory to you. You judge the architect by him building a building, you judge the musician by the performance of a piece. And similarly, you can’t judge the philosopher by their ability to speak wonderfully about the virtuous life, but rather to live the virtuous life and to demonstrate it when those things do happen to them. Was that also Seneca, or was that someone else?

John Cellers: It probably appears in Seneca somewhere — he writes a lot. Epictetus certainly talks about that. And another Roman Stoic who was around at this time, a contemporary of Seneca, who taught Epictetus — a chap called Musonius Rufus. He’s less well known, but well worth reading, so I’d encourage people to have a look at Musonius Rufus. In one passage he says, if you had a choice between being treated by a doctor who was very good at giving lectures on medical theory, or someone who had experience in the field of actually stitching people back up, which would you pick to treat you? You know which one you’d pick — you’d pick the one who was expert in practice.

Alex O’Connor: Even if they were bad at the theory. Because it’s not just that — imagine a doctor who’s really good at theoretical explanation but has a track record of really bad practice, and patients dying, versus someone who sounds completely confused about medicine when they try to talk to you but has this perfect track record. You’d obviously pick the track record. And with philosophy, perhaps it should be the same thing.

Fate, Determinism, and Free Will

Alex O’Connor: What’s annoying is that this is such a beautiful, wonderful place to wrap up and end, but I feel like I have to ask, just briefly — which is so much less satisfying a conclusion. You mentioned earlier about how these things are in our control, we can choose how to react to the world. But just before that, you were talking about fate, and about the fact that the Stoics believe in this material series of cause and effect, and how the universe eternally recurs and occurs in the same way every time. There’s always that shipwreck. If that’s the case, how can I say that I can choose my outcome if everything’s determined? Doesn’t that remove the ability for human free will, which is kind of a requirement of the Stoic response to the world?

John Cellers: Yeah, really good question, really good big meaty philosophical problem. The way in which the Stoics would describe it — it’s very, very difficult, isn’t it? Strictly speaking, given that they’re determinists, the Stoics can’t believe in free will in the sense that we have the ability to choose otherwise. Nevertheless, they’re going to argue that we still make our own choices. What we choose to do right now is a choice that we make based on the current circumstances we find ourselves in and who we are. There’s a sense in which the nexus of fate will work itself out, and there will be one outcome from that. There’s a sense in which that is predetermined, but of course we don’t know.

But the important point is that we are involved in the process. One of our sources describes this as saying that fate doesn’t do things to us — fate works through us. We are one of the parties involved. To give an example: let’s say you encountered someone attacking an animal, or something like that. Now, you might be the sort of person who will go over and intervene and say, “What on earth are you doing?” You might be someone who will shy away because you want to avoid the confrontation. There’s a sense in which which of those things you do, what you finally do, is going to be determined by the sort of person that you are, and the past experiences that you’ve had, and the very specific details of that context — how big is the perpetrator? All of these things will be involved in you making that decision. There’s a sense in which, if we had all the relevant bits of information, we could predict what you would do. If we know you’re a fervent paid-up member of the RSPCA, we can be highly confident you’re going to intervene, because we know who you are. To the point that we would say, well, there’s no question that he would just walk away and turn a blind eye — that’s just not who he is, he couldn’t do that. So in that sense, we could say it’s going to be determined in some sense.

Alex O’Connor: It sounds like a kind of compatibilism, essentially.

John Cellers: That’s precisely the word that academics will use to describe their view, yes.

Alex O’Connor: Interesting. That’s certainly how it sounds. I’m not a huge fan of compatibilism, but I do find it interesting how, on this free will question — I give the example of a firefighter running into a burning building, and everyone afterwards saying, “Oh, you’re such a hero, why did you do that, why did you put yourself at risk?” And they say something like, “I had to, I couldn’t live with myself if I didn’t. I didn’t even think, I just ran in there.” On one hand, when we’re doing philosophy, we want to say there’s this problem: if people don’t act freely, then how can they act morally? They seem to be in conflict. And yet in a circumstance like that, when someone says, “I didn’t even think, I just had to, I just went in there,” in practice we see that as more virtuous. We’re more impressed by that. We think, wow, what an incredibly moral person. I suppose this puts the virtue in virtue ethics. It’s not that you take each individual decision and figure out what’s going to maximize utility. It’s just that if you are a virtuous person, you just will behave in particular ways, and how you behave will either be more in line with nature and the truth about the world, or more out of line with nature and thereby just false. So it’s just true or false behavior, stemming necessarily from your true or false understanding of the nature of the world.

John Cellers: Yeah, which brings us right back to character. That is important, so we’re very much in the realm of virtue ethics.
